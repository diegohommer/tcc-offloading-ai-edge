Energy Cost of Confidence-Based Offloading in Hierarchical LLM Inference over Passive Optical Networks

What it costs, in joules, to serve LLM inference through a confidence-based recursive cascade on a four-tier PON — and what actually governs that cost.

00Abstract

Large language model inference is moving off centralised datacentres and onto tiered infrastructure that reaches the network edge. Passive optical networks are the access technology this traffic will cross, and recent work has proposed mapping a four-tier LLM inference continuum — user device, ONU, OLT, cloud — directly onto TWDM-PON. That architecture inherits its offloading rule from RecServe: each tier answers locally, scores its own confidence, and escalates one tier up when confidence falls below a threshold calibrated from the β-quantile of its recent history. Neither the architecture nor the offloading rule has ever been evaluated in terms of energy.

This work supplies that evaluation. It assembles a per-tier energy characterisation from verified literature plus first-party RAPL measurements, instruments a real four-tier cascade to produce per-query traces, and prices those traces in joules. Its central result is analytical and negative: weighting the confidence threshold by an energy cost is redundant with the β parameter RecServe already exposes, because a quantile is a rank statistic and tier-constant terms cannot re-rank queries. Energy-awareness in this architecture is therefore a matter of configuration — which model sits on which tier, and where β is set — not of the escalation rule.

01Context and Motivation

Inference, not training, now dominates the lifetime energy cost of deployed language models. As inference distributes toward the edge, the question of where a query should be computed becomes an energy question as much as a latency question — and the two do not have the same answer.

Passive optical networks are the relevant substrate. PON is the dominant fibre access technology, it is already deployed at scale, and its topology maps naturally onto a compute hierarchy: customer premises equipment, the ONU at the subscriber, the OLT at the central office, and the operator's cloud beyond. Pakpahan and Hwang [2] make that mapping explicit, proposing an SDN-controlled tiered LLM inference continuum over TWDM-PON with declared model-size bands and hardware classes per tier.

Their evaluation, however, is an OPNET network simulation — latency, jitter, throughput, packet drop. No language model is executed, no confidence score is computed, and no joule is counted. The offloading decision itself is imported from RecServe [1]. The gap this work addresses sits precisely there: the architecture has a declared energy motivation and no energy evaluation.

02Reference Works

2.1RecServe — the offloading mechanism

RecServe [1] performs recursive offloading across a multi-tier network. Each tier runs local inference, computes a task-specific confidence score, and compares it against a threshold T(β) derived from the β-quantile of a sliding window of its own recent confidences. Above threshold, the local answer is returned; below, the query escalates exactly one tier. The rule is decentralised by design: no tier reads another tier's state.

Its published evaluation covers three tiers and encoder models of 60M–355M parameters on classification tasks. For generative tasks it specifies normalised perplexity as the confidence measure.

2.2Pakpahan and Hwang — the architecture

The reference architecture [2] defines four tiers with explicit parameter bands and hardware classes, reproduced below. These bands are the fidelity constraint this work's experimental cascade is held to.

03Problem Statement and Research Questions

A confidence-based recursive cascade trades answer quality against the cost of escalation. The cost side of that trade has never been quantified for this architecture, and the offloading rule that governs it contains no energy term.

  1. RQ1 — Characterisation. What is the per-tier energy cost of LLM inference across a four-tier PON hierarchy, in joules per token, with each figure traceable to a measurement on hardware of that tier's declared class?
  2. RQ2 — The frontier. What accuracy–energy frontier does the confidence-based cascade trace, priced in measured joules, and where on it does the cost of escalation begin to exceed its quality return?
  3. RQ3 — The rule. Can the escalation rule be made energy-aware by weighting its confidence threshold with a per-tier energy cost, and does doing so improve on tuning β alone?
  4. RQ4 — Transport. What share of total cascade energy is access-network transport rather than computation, and under what workload does that share stop being negligible?

04Scope

This is an evaluation, not a proposal for a new mechanism. Three boundaries follow from that, and are stated here rather than discovered later.

  • Energy is priced, not measured end to end. Per-tier joules-per-token figures come from published measurements on the declared hardware class, plus first-party RAPL measurements on the machine running the cascade. Real traces supply routing decisions and token counts; the tables supply the cost.
  • The cascade is real, the deployment is not. Models are executed and confidences computed for every tier on every query. No TWDM-PON testbed is involved; transport energy is modelled from published ONU power figures.
  • The mechanism is RecServe's, unmodified in structure. This work evaluates and characterises that mechanism. Where it substitutes a component — the confidence definition, §6.4 — the substitution is drawn from published work and cited as such.

05Methodology

5.1Energy accounting

The metric requires more care than it first appears to, and getting it wrong changes conclusions rather than decimals. Energy per token is a ratio, and its denominator is not fixed:

J/token_reported = E_total / n_gen = (E_prefill(n_prompt) + e_decode · n_gen) / n_gen = e_decode + E_prefill / n_gen ^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^ task-dependent, not hardware-dependent

Prefill is paid once and divided by however many tokens the task generates. A benchmark that emits a handful of tokens reports a per-token figure inflated by unamortised prefill; one that emits hundreds reports a figure close to pure decode. The effect is large enough to invert comparisons:

Same model, same GPU class, three tasks — Bench360 [7]
ModelTaskGenerated tokensJ/token
Gemma-2-9B FP16MMLU~1–54.65
Gemma-2-9B FP16SQuAD v2short span4.12
Gemma-2-9B FP16CNN/DM~50–1001.75

Convention adopted. Following Solovyeva and Castor [3] and the emerging standard represented by TokenPowerBench [8], energy is reported by phase, with distinct denominators — input tokens for prefill, output tokens for decode:

E_query = E_pf_per_token · n_prompt + E_dec_per_token · n_gen

This is the form the costing module already implements, and as of September 2026 it is populated rather than approximated: a per-tier prefill energy is tabulated, measured for the user and ONU tiers and derived from published ratios for fog and cloud (§7.1). It also exposes a structural property of stepwise escalation that a single blended figure hides: escalation re-runs prefill at every tier, since no KV cache crosses a tier boundary. A query reaching cloud has paid prefill four times — and with roughly a thousand prompt tokens against 34–90 generated, that is a first-order cost rather than a rounding term.

Known approximation

The additive form is an approximation. Solovyeva and Castor [3] show the phases are coupled — higher prefill cost amplifies per-token decode cost, plausibly through KV-cache pressure on a memory-bound phase. The residual is bounded by the same evidence that bounds prefill's share (§7.1) and is reported, not assumed away.

Four tiers are instantiated with decoder models, each inside its declared band, each with published energy on its tier's hardware class, and each served at the precision that energy was measured at. The precision ladder is the architecture, not a confound to be removed. The reference architecture specifies quantised execution at the edge — it is what makes an ONU-class accelerator viable — and full precision in the datacentre. Serving every tier at one precision would model a handset running BF16, which no handset does, and would sever the correspondence with the user and ONU energy measurements, which exist only at W4. The precision step is a substantial part of why tiers differ in joules per token, and it is modelled rather than eliminated.

The consequence for interpretation is that a rung's quality gain mixes two causes — larger model and higher numerical precision. That is the quantity the deployment actually delivers, and it is the one this work measures; it is not a controlled comparison of capacity at fixed precision, and is not reported as one.

The cloud tier is not executed locally. Its per-instance results are taken from the Open LLM Leaderboard archive, which ran the model unquantised on datacentre hardware — the same precision as its energy source. Because the top tier is terminal under a stepwise rule, it never thresholds its own confidence, so accuracy and token counts are all that is required of it.

5.4Workload and collection design

The task is GSM8K chain-of-thought reasoning — chosen because it is decode-dominated (median 170–230 generated tokens, the regime the energy tables actually measure) and because it carries an objective per-query difficulty label, which enables the analysis in §6.5.

Collection is exhaustive rather than path-dependent: every tier answers every query, and the full per-token log-probability vector is stored alongside the generated text, at temperature 0. Escalation is path-dependent — β changes which tiers a query visits, which changes each tier's confidence history — so a single cascade trace cannot answer what a skipped tier would have said. Running the full matrix once makes every downstream question (any β, any λ, any confidence definition, any energy model) answerable offline, without re-executing a model.

06Results to Date

6.1The accuracy–energy frontier exists and has a sharp knee established

Most of the saving lands before the accuracy cost becomes visible. Three weighting forms — additive, multiplicative, exponential — were measured and trace the same frontier; the exponential form is adopted on the engineering merit of being bounded in (0, T(β)] without an arbitrary clamp.

6.2The energy term is redundant with β established · central result

The proposed rule sets an effective threshold T(β) · exp(−λ · cost). When cost is constant per tier, the exponential is a constant k, and k · T(β) is simply another quantile of the same distribution — that is, T(β′) for some β′. Scaling a quantile by a constant is indistinguishable from choosing a different β.

Three variants designed to escape that algebra were measured on the generative cascade (GSM8K, n=200), each compared against the Pareto frontier of tuning β alone:

VariantHow cost variesMean gain over β alone
Static costConstant per tier pair+0.0039
Per-hop costDiffers between user→onu and onu→fog+0.0012
Per-query costProportional to response length+0.0026

All gains are within noise at this sample size. The generalisation is the contribution: under a quantile-derived threshold, any criterion that transforms confidence using tier-constant parameters is behaviourally identical to some choice of β, because a quantile is a rank statistic and such terms do not re-rank queries. The per-query variant does re-rank — but on response length, which carries no information about whether escalating would help. Energy says what a hop costs; it does not say whether the hop is worth taking.

The constructive reading

Redundancy runs both ways. β is the energy dial — RecServe simply never says how to set it. β is sufficient to reach any point on the frontier; per-tier energy measurement is necessary to locate the desired point, since escalation rate is not a proxy for consumption. With an oversized ONU (14B, 1.89 J/token) the same mechanism reaches 20.1 J/query at baseline and the optimal operating point shifts by an order of magnitude.

6.3Non-monotonic ladders corrupt stepwise recursion established

On GSM8K, SOLAR-10.7B is worse than the smaller Llama-3.1-8B (0.675 vs 0.860). Paired per-query, escalating onu→fog repairs 7 answers and breaks 44. Because escalation is stepwise, a query bound for a higher tier must traverse the inverted one — so a bad rung does not merely waste its own energy, it converts that energy into wrong answers and degrades every tier above it. This is invisible at the 60M–355M scale of RecServe's own evaluation.

6.4The specified confidence measure is not the best available established · replication

Four confidence definitions were compared by Welch t separating correct from incorrect answers, on three tiers spanning an 8× size range. exp(min logprob) — the weakest token — separates 10–50% better than the normalised-perplexity measure RecServe specifies (t = 6.36 / 7.16 / 5.09 versus 4.62 / 4.75 / 4.86). A wrong answer betrays itself by its worst token, not by its average fluency.

This is a replication, and is reported as one: Gupta et al. [11] formalise exactly these estimators for LM cascades as Chow-Sum, Chow-Average and Chow-Quantile, with α = 0 defined as the minimum token log-probability, and show quantile-based deferral improving on the length-biased simple aggregations. The contribution here is that the result transfers to a recursive multi-tier cascade with measured energy — a setting their work does not cover.

Accuracy falls by a factor of three; confidence varies by under 0.01 and on this tier drifts upward. The pattern holds identically at 1B, 8B and 10.7B, so it is a property of perplexity-based confidence rather than of scale. The consequence for routing is direct, and it is the same root cause as §6.2: escalation catches wrong answers but cannot preferentially route the genuinely hard ones. The underlying calibration phenomenon — the hard–easy effect — is documented independently by Michael et al. [12]; the contribution here is its consequence for stepwise cascade routing.

6.6Access-network transport is seven orders of magnitude below computation established

A query moves 4.2 KB — a median 1022-token prompt plus a 49-token answer, at four bytes per token, with the whole prompt re-sent at each hop because no KV cache crosses a tier boundary. On the 25 Gb/s-per-wavelength TWDM-PON the reference architecture specifies, that is 1.4 µs of transmission; at the ONU's 3.98 W that is 5.5 µJ.

There is no crossover to locate. Even legacy GPON at 1.244 Gb/s upstream, twenty times slower, leaves transport five orders below computation. An earlier version of this section put the share at 0.1–1% and treated the crossover as something still to be found; that estimate rested on a flat 0.1 J-per-hop constant rather than on bytes moved, and is withdrawn.

Why PON makes this categorical

A PON is passive: no powered element sits in the distribution network, only an optical splitter. All energy is at the two endpoints — the subscriber's ONU and the OLT port — and both are always on, drawing power whether or not anything is transmitted. Transport energy therefore splits into two quantities that the old constant conflated. The marginal cost of one more query is transmission time times power, 5.5 µJ, and it is the figure the escalation decision should use, because the ONU is powered regardless. The amortised share of always-on power is 3.98 W divided by the household's query rate, which spans 0.10 J at forty queries per second to 14.3 kJ at one per hour — governed by household load, not by the cascade. The retired 0.1 J constant was the amortised figure at 39.8 queries per second per ONU, a load assumption that was never written down.

The claim this supports inverts the premise of the classical mobile-edge-computing literature, where local computation is traded against transmission energy: for LLM inference over PON that trade has collapsed. A query moves kilobytes and burns hundreds of joules. Energy-aware offloading here is a compute-placement problem, not a networking one — which is also why no per-query energy signal could beat β (§6.2): the cost is dominated by static per-tier configuration. The corollary is that what matters on a PON is time powered rather than traffic, making the ONU sleep state (0.4 W against 3.98 W) the access-network energy lever, on a design axis the offloading decision never touches.

7.1Phase reporting is inconsistent across sources

The user and ONU sources report decode isolated from prefill, matching the convention adopted in §5.1. The fog and cloud sources report blended figures. That gap has since been closed from the other direction: a per-tier prefill energy is now tabulated, measured for the user and ONU tiers and derived from published ratios for fog and cloud, so the two-term model is populated rather than approximated.

An earlier version of this section bounded prefill at ≤3.4% of total energy, citing figures drawn from workloads that emit around 300 tokens. That bound does not transfer to this work's regime, which emits 34–90 tokens against roughly a thousand of prompt, and it is withdrawn. The measured shares are: user 85%, fog 17%, ONU 8%, cloud 3%. The user tier is prefill-dominated, because it is the only tier that decodes on a CPU — prefill is compute-bound and decode is memory-bound, so the backend that is weakest at compute pays the most for prefill. The prefill:decode ratio accordingly spans 1:4.6 on the handset CPU to 1:370 on datacentre GPUs, which is mechanism rather than measurement noise.

Correcting this moved the frontier substantially, and not where it was expected to. Pricing both terms raises energy by 19% at the expensive end and by 432% at the cheap end, because at low β almost every query stops at the prefill-dominated user tier. The accuracy–energy frontier compresses from an 83× energy span to 18×: the savings available from offloading are materially smaller than a decode-only accounting suggests, and the cheap end is where the correction bites hardest.

7.2Batch regimes differ across tiers

User and ONU figures are batch-1; fog and cloud figures come from batched serving (Watt Counts uses 1000-query batches). This partly reflects reality — a handset does run batch-1 and a datacentre does not — but it is a modelling decision and is declared as one rather than left implicit.

7.3Sample size and scope

The generative results rest on n=200. An earlier n=40 run produced a qualitatively wrong picture, not merely a noisier one — the capability ladder appeared inverted — so small-n results are excluded rather than caveated. No specific λ or β value transfers across hardware ladders; what transfers is the calibration procedure.

7.4Confidence and energy refer to the same model at approximately, not exactly, the same bit-width

The two halves of each tier are joined by model name, but the join is looser than the name suggests. Confidence traces are collected through Ollama, which applies post-training weight-only quantisation and serves Q4_K_M by default — a k-quant with mixed precision per tensor. The energy tables cite W4 figures produced by AWQ- or GPTQ-style schemes; and on the ONU tier's Jetson Orin, the W4 path falls back to INT8 arithmetic, because the Ampere architecture has no INT4 support [5]. All three are "4-bit". None is the same algorithm.

This matters because quantisation changes the weights, and therefore the output distribution, and therefore the confidence — it is not a rounding effect. Under greedy chain-of-thought decoding the divergence compounds: a single flipped argmax early in a 170–230 token trace produces a different reasoning chain from that point on, so the confidence being scored belongs to a different answer rather than to a slightly perturbed one.

The magnitude is measurable, and measured on exactly these models. Husom et al. [14] evaluate 28 quantised LLMs drawn from the same Ollama library — including llama3.2:1b-instruct-q4_K_M and qwen2.5:1.5b-instruct-q4_K_M, this work's user and ONU candidates — against hardware power measurement, and find accuracy varying significantly across quantisation levels, with the worst degradation in mathematical reasoning tasks: the regime this work's GSM8K workload sits in. Energy across the same range varies by up to 79% between FP16 and the q3/q4 variants.

The requirement this imposes is within a tier, not across them: what must agree is the precision at which a tier's confidence is collected and the precision at which its energy was measured. Under that test the ladder in §5.3 scores unevenly, and the two ends of it invert the expectation:

The cloud tier, the one this work cannot execute, turns out to be the best-matched: the leaderboard ran the model unquantised and its energy was measured unquantised, so both sides refer to the same weights. The fog tier is the one that requires care — its energy figure is an FP16 measurement, so serving Gemma-2-9B through a 4-bit local runtime would introduce a wider mismatch than exists at the edge tiers, where at least both sides are 4-bit. FP16 weights for a 9B model are roughly 18.5 GB, which fits the available memory, so this gap is closable by serving choice rather than by caveat.

A correction to how tightly the fog claim can be made. Bench360 does not state the serving scenario behind its energy table, and it reports figures for three 24 GB GPUs without saying which row ran on which. Implied power — energy per token divided by seconds per token — is a near-constant 94–116 W across every model and task in that table, and the latency metric reported is TPOT, a per-request quantity: both are single-stream signatures, so the regime is inferred rather than declared. That same ~107 W also exceeds the L4's 72 W TDP, which places those rows on the A10 (150 W) or A30 (165 W) rather than the L4. This work's fog tier was collected on a Modal L4, so the correspondence claimed here is to the declared hardware class, not to an identical chip.

What remains irreducible is the edge pair: Q4_K_M against AWQ/GPTQ-style W4 is a difference of quantisation algorithm, and closing it would require measuring energy on the exact quantisation served — which is not possible without owning a Snapdragon and a Jetson. That residual is the honest bound on how tightly the correspondence can be claimed, and it applies to two tiers rather than to all four.

08Positioning

Threshold-based quality–energy trade-offs are not novel; EcoThink [13] formulates a structurally similar constrained optimisation, and the pattern is standard in green-computing literature. Three things are specific to this work.

  • The setting. A physically distributed multi-tier PON cascade with heterogeneous hardware, rather than depth selection inside a single system.
  • The evidence base. Measured energy from independent profiling sources plus first-party RAPL, rather than analytical estimates from manufacturer TDP.
  • The negative result. None of the five mechanisms Pakpahan and Hwang compare — Tabi, PerLLM, EdgeShard, Draft–Verify, RecServe — is energy-aware. This work shows why adding energy-awareness to the threshold cannot work, which is more useful than another mechanism that does not.
