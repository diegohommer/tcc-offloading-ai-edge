\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}
%==============================================================================

As the use of Large Language Models (LLMs) becomes widespread, both at work and at
home~\cite{bick2026rapid}, the challenge of adapting the network architecture to handle 
this rapidly evolving technology remains an active research
topic~\cite{chen2026network}. Since evolution seldom happens in isolation, the network
itself has also been undergoing changes, one of which is the adoption of Passive Optical
Network (PON) technology in the access network~\cite{effenberger2007introduction}. Driven
by the demand for greater capacity, lower latency, and reduced operating costs, PON is
being deployed alongside or in place of the Hybrid Fiber-Coaxial (HFC)
plant~\cite{prodnik2025migration}. Quality of service, scalability, security, and energy
cost are some of the broad topics that need to be taken into account as both of these
changes happen in parallel.

On the models' side, cost is a concern as well, since LLM inference is
expensive~\cite{chen2026network}, and one way of making it cheaper is the model cascade,
in which a smaller model answers what it can and passes a query to a larger one only when
its answer is not reliable enough~\cite{chen2024frugalgpt}. What counts as expensive
depends on the setting, from the price of an API call to the energy spent answering
each query, and energy is the cost this work is concerned with. Treating it as a cost
of its own is the idea behind Green AI, which argues that efficiency should be reported alongside
accuracy~\cite{schwartz2020green}, and some serving systems already use it to decide
which model answers a query. GreenServ, for instance, picks a model from a heterogeneous
pool based on the accuracy and energy it has observed, and uses 31\% less energy than
routing at random~\cite{ziller2026greenserv}.

These systems choose which model answers, but not where it runs, which is a separate
question. With edge computing, inference is no longer confined to the cloud, but spread
across end devices, edge nodes, and cloud servers, each tier further from the user than
the one below, and with more computing power~\cite{chen2026network}. RecServe puts the two ideas together, placing a cascade on
the tiers of such a network and letting each tier decide, from the confidence scores it
has seen recently, whether to answer the query or to pass it up~\cite{wu2025recserve}.
Following RecServe's decision rule, \citeonline{pakpahan2026continuum} bring tiered
LLM inference into the access network itself, equipping Optical Network Units (ONUs)
and Optical Line Terminals (OLTs) to run part of the inference, so that a Time and
Wavelength Division Multiplexed PON (TWDM-PON) provides the edge and fog tiers of
such a hierarchy. In both works, however, a tier decides whether to pass a query up
from confidence alone, and the energy spent at each tier plays no part in that
decision.

In this work, we propose the integration of energy awareness into recursive tiered LLM
inference, so that each tier decides whether to answer a query or pass it up from the
confidence of its answer and also from the energy expected to be spent at each tier. As a
case study, we simulate a three-tier
hierarchy over a PON, formed by the user device, the ONU and the OLT, each running a
larger model than the one below. We evaluate two energy-aware strategies, one that
follows a fixed timetable of the OLT's cost and one that takes advantage of the PON
downstream broadcast, with the OLT periodically announcing its current cost to every
ONU. Both are compared with escalation by confidence alone at the same target accuracy, in energy per query
and in communication burden, under recorded conversation traffic with and without
unforeseen surges. \todo{one or two sentences with the main results, once the Results
chapter is settled.}

The contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The integration of energy awareness into recursive tiered LLM inference, in which the
  decision to answer a query or pass it up takes into account the energy spent at each
  tier, and not only confidence;
  \item An energy model of the user device, the ONU and the OLT under a single
  measurement boundary, with the OLT's energy measured by us on a Graphics Processing Unit (GPU);
  \item An offloading strategy that uses the PON downstream broadcast to share the
  OLT's current cost with every ONU;
  \item A simulation study on recorded conversation traffic, with and without
  unforeseen surges, comparing the energy-aware strategies with confidence-only
  escalation in energy per query and communication burden.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Passive Optical Networks}
\label{sec:pon}
A PON is an access network technology that uses a shared fiber architecture, where a
single fiber coming out of an OLT is split passively to serve several dozen ONUs at the
subscribers' premises, up to 128 of them in Gigabit-capable PON (G-PON). Because the
fiber is shared, the OLT downstream works as a broadcast, with the splitters passively
replicating its signal across all the ONUs connected to it, and each ONU looks at the
frame headers and keeps only the data addressed to it, discarding the
rest~\cite{effenberger2007introduction}.

In the opposite direction, the ONUs share the fiber through Time Division Multiple
Access (TDMA) to avoid collisions, with the OLT assigning each ONU strictly synchronized
time slots through Dynamic Bandwidth Allocation (DBA), so that the ONUs take turns
bursting data back~\cite{effenberger2007introduction}. The two directions are therefore
not symmetric, because anything the OLT sends reaches every ONU at once while each ONU
can only speak in its own slot (Figure~\ref{fig:pon}).

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \caption{Downstream broadcast and upstream TDMA in a PON}
  \label{fig:pon}
  \vspace{6pt}
  \input{figures/pon_broadcast_tdma}
  \legend{Source: Adapted from \cite{roonie2013pon}}
\end{figure}

More recently, TWDM-PON, selected as the primary solution for the Next-Generation PON Stage 2 (NG-PON2), stacked several wavelength pairs on
top of the time sharing, each pair running its own TDMA, to multiply the capacity of the
same fiber~\cite{luo2013twdm}, and it is the setting in which
\citeonline{pakpahan2026continuum} place tiered LLM inference
(Section~\ref{sec:pakpahan}). One of the strategies evaluated in this work relies on
the downstream broadcast, since a single message from the OLT reaches every ONU with no
extra upstream traffic (Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}).

\subsection{LLM Inference}
\label{sec:llm-background}

LLMs are scaled Pre-trained Language Models (PLMs), Transformer models with
self-attention mechanisms~\cite{vaswani2017attention} that are pre-trained on large-scale
text corpora and solve a wide range of tasks using natural language. Researchers noticed
that the more a PLM is scaled, in model size or in training data, the more capable it
tends to become at problem solving, and large PLMs also show abilities that smaller ones
lack, such as solving a new task from a few examples given in the
prompt~\cite{zhao2023survey}. The same size, however, places heavy demands on memory,
computation and energy when these models are served~\cite{chen2026network}.

These demands come from how an LLM answers a query, which happens in two
phases~\cite{zhou2024survey}. In the prefill phase, the LLM processes the whole user prompt at once, storing
intermediate results for every prompt token (the Key-Value, or KV, cache) and generating
the first output token. In the decode phase, it generates the rest of the answer one
token at a time, each step reusing the KV cache and adding the new token to it
(Figure~\ref{fig:prefill-decode}). The two phases use the hardware differently, as prefill processes many tokens in
parallel and tends to be limited by computation, while each decode step processes a single token
but moves about as much data as prefill, which makes it limited by memory
bandwidth~\cite{zhong2024distserve}. For this reason, the energy of a query is often
reported separately for each phase (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}).

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \caption{Prefill and decode phases of LLM inference}
  \label{fig:prefill-decode}
  \vspace{6pt}
  \input{figures/prefill_decode}
  \legend{Source: Adapted from \cite{zhou2024survey,zhong2024distserve}}
\end{figure}

To generate each token, the LLM assigns a probability to every token in its vocabulary,
and a low probability on the tokens it picks is a sign that it is unsure of the
answer~\cite{zhao2023survey}. A confidence score turns these probabilities into
a single number per answer, and for generated text the most common scores combine the
probabilities of all the generated tokens, for example through their average, their
minimum, or the likelihood of the whole answer normalized by its
length~\cite{geng2023survey}. Perplexity, closely related to the last one, is the
inverse probability of a sequence normalized by its number of tokens, so the more likely
the answer, the lower its perplexity~\cite[ch.~3]{jurafsky2026speech}.

Ideally, a score is calibrated, meaning that answers given with 80\% confidence are
correct 80\% of the time, but modern neural networks are often poorly calibrated and
tend to be more confident than they are accurate~\cite{guo2017calibration}. A fixed
threshold on such a score would therefore have to be tuned by hand for each task, model
and network condition, so a model can instead compare each confidence with the ones it
has seen recently. It keeps the confidences of its last $k$ answers and accepts an answer
whose confidence reaches their $\beta$-quantile, so about a fraction $\beta$ of its
answers are rejected by construction, whatever the scale of the score. This test was
proposed by \citeonline{wu2025recserve}, with a normalized perplexity, the geometric
mean of the probabilities of the answer's tokens, as the confidence of generated text,
and we refer to it as the quantile test.

\subsection{Model Cascades and Tiered Inference}
\label{sec:cascades-tiers}

A model cascade places several models one after the other, from the cheapest to the most
expensive, and sends each query to the cheapest one first. If that model's answer is
reliable, it is returned and the more expensive models are never queried, and otherwise
the query moves on to the next model in the list. FrugalGPT judges reliability with a separate scoring function that gives
each answer a score between 0 and 1, and with it matches the performance of GPT-4 at up
to 98\% lower cost~\cite{chen2024frugalgpt}. Cascades also differ in how that
judgement is made, and in AutoMix the smaller model checks its own answer through
few-shot self-verification, without extensive training. Since this check can be noisy, a router then decides from the
resulting confidence whether to move up to a larger model, reducing the computational
cost by over 50\% at comparable performance~\cite{aggarwal2023automix}.

The idea of a model cascade was also brought into the network itself, whose tiers go
from the user's own device, through the edge and the fog, up to the cloud, with a larger,
more capable LLM at each step up~\cite{pakpahan2026continuum}. A query then climbs the
tiers only as far as it needs, so simpler queries are answered before they reach the
cloud, closer to the user, where the shorter network path lowers
latency~\cite{chen2026network}. Fewer queries crossing the network also means less
traffic, and a cascade over the device, the edge and the cloud that escalates with the
quantile test reduces the communication burden by over 50\% compared with serving every
query in the cloud~\cite{wu2025recserve}.

\subsection{Measuring LLM Inference Energy}
\label{sec:metrics}
\label{sec:measurements}

Studies report the energy of LLM inference per generated token or separately for the
prefill and decode phases~\cite{solovyeva2026green}, or per
query~\cite{elsworth2025measuring,chung2025mlenergy}, and there is no agreed way of
measuring any of them. Of these units, this work compares tiers per query, since different
models can answer the same prompt with responses of very different lengths, and a figure
per token would hide how verbose each model is~\cite{chung2025mlenergy}. Each query's
energy is still built from the two phases, as the energy per prompt token in the prefill
plus the energy per generated token in the decode (Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}).

What a figure means depends on its measurement boundary, the set of components whose
energy it counts, and a boundary can cover only the accelerator or also the host's CPU
and memory, the idle machines kept ready for peaks, and the overhead of the building
itself. That
overhead is measured by the Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE), the ratio between a
facility's total energy and the energy of its computing equipment. In Google's
accounting of a median prompt, the accelerators take 58\% of the energy, the host's CPU
and memory 25\%, idle machines 10\% and the data center's overhead 8\%. Since there is
no consensus on which of these to count, published estimates for similar tasks can be an
order of magnitude apart~\cite{elsworth2025measuring}. GPUs account for 50--70\% of a
data center's provisioned power~\cite{chung2025mlenergy}, so measuring only the GPU
leaves out a large part of a query's energy, and figures from different studies cannot
be compared directly.

Servers usually answer several queries at once, and batching them together amortizes
fixed costs such as memory transfers, although its effect on energy depends on the
inference phase and on the setup~\cite{delavande2026understanding}. Since an LLM
generates its answer one token per step, the queries in a batch finish at different
times. Earlier serving systems kept the batch fixed until every query in it was done, so
a finished query could not return to the user and a new one had to wait. Orca instead
schedules one step at a time, so queries join and leave the batch between
steps~\cite{yu2022orca}. vLLM batches the same way and also stores the KV cache in
fixed-size blocks, so more queries fit in a batch. Its throughput is 2--4 times that of
Orca and FasterTransformer at the same latency~\cite{kwon2023vllm}. We refer to this way
of serving as continuous batching.

Because a query's energy then depends on the other queries in its batch, there is more
than one way to charge it. The GPU reports only the energy of the whole batch, and the
rules used in practice to split it among queries can differ substantially from a fair
split, with static and continuous batching alike~\cite{luo2026request}. Average
accounting charges each query its share of the batch's energy, while marginal accounting
charges only what the query adds to it (Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}).

The boundary also depends on the tool, since each class of hardware is measured
differently. GPUs report their own energy in software, read through the NVIDIA
Management Library (NVML) on the NVIDIA GPUs this work uses, while libraries such as
Zeus, used by \citeonline{chung2025mlenergy}, read it on both NVIDIA and AMD GPUs.
These readings have limits of their own, since on the A100 and H100 the power that
nvidia-smi reports samples only a quarter of the runtime, which can lead to a large
under- or overestimate of the energy spent~\cite{yang2023part}. Phones expose on-chip
telemetry that reports the energy of the whole chip~\cite{cai2026npu}, while single-board
edge devices are measured either at the plug, with a power meter that includes the whole
system~\cite{renney2026cloud}, or with current sensors on the board's power
rails~\cite{tummalapalli2026edge}. Since each tool covers a different part of the
system, Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} converts every tier of this work to a single
boundary.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Tiered LLM Inference}
\label{sec:tiered}

The works closest to ours place a cascade of LLMs on the tiers of a network.
\label{sec:recserve}
RecServe~\cite{wu2025recserve} deploys progressively larger models on the end device,
the edge node and the cloud, and every query starts at the device. Each tier keeps the
confidence scores of its last $k$ answers and sets its threshold at their
$\beta$-quantile. An answer whose confidence reaches the threshold goes back to the
user, and otherwise the query moves to the next tier, which repeats the test until the
query reaches the cloud. The threshold follows each tier's own recent scores, so it needs no
manual tuning, and each tier passes up roughly a fraction $\beta$ of its queries. On
sentiment classification and translation tasks, RecServe reaches accuracies close to
those of the cloud alone, with a better trade-off between accuracy and communication
than a cascade with fixed, hand-tuned thresholds. RecServe's theoretical
analysis considers the cost of each tier, but only as a fixed number to guide the choice
of $\beta$, and each query is still decided on its confidence alone.

\label{sec:pakpahan}
\citeonline{pakpahan2026continuum} bring this hierarchy into the access network, mapping
four tiers onto a TWDM-PON (Figure~\ref{fig:pakpahan}). Tiny LLMs run on user devices,
quantized models on ONUs equipped with an accelerator, models of 7 to 13 billion
parameters on OLTs at the central office, and larger models in the cloud. Most of their
work is on the network side, where a Software-Defined Networking (SDN) controller gives
inference traffic its own priority queue in the DBA and switches on a second wavelength
under congestion, reducing average inference latency by up to 50\% in simulation. Each
tier decides whether to serve a request or pass it up with RecServe's rule, so here too
the decision rests on confidence alone.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \caption{Tiered LLM inference continuum on a PON}
  \label{fig:pakpahan}
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/pakpahan2026_fig1_tiered_continuum.jpg}
  \legend{Source: \cite{pakpahan2026continuum}}
\end{figure}

The other edge--cloud methods compared by \citeauthoronline{pakpahan2026continuum} split the
work differently. Tabi~\cite{wang2023tabi} is a two-model cascade within one server,
passing a query to the larger model when the smaller one's calibrated confidence falls
below a threshold profiled offline for each task. EdgeShard~\cite{zhang2024edgeshard}
splits a single LLM into shards of layers placed on edge devices and a cloud server, so
every query runs the whole model and there is no decision to make per query. In the
draft--verify method of \citeonline{hao2024hybrid}, a small model at the edge generates
blocks of tokens that a large model in the cloud verifies, replacing the tokens it finds
unlikely. It reaches the quality of the large model on GSM8K at 25.8\% of its cost,
although every block needs a round trip to the cloud.

Of the works above, only RecServe and the architecture of
\citeauthoronline{pakpahan2026continuum} decide at every tier whether to answer a query
or pass it up, and both decide from confidence alone, so none of these works, those two
included, uses the energy spent at each tier to decide where a query is answered.

\subsection{Energy-Aware LLM Serving and Routing}
\label{sec:energy-routing}

Other works bring energy into the decision itself, choosing where, or by which model, a
query is answered. PerLLM~\cite{yang2024perllm} schedules LLM requests between edge servers running small
models and a cloud server running a larger one. A central scheduler assigns each request
to one server so as to minimize transmission, inference and idle energy while meeting
its deadline, reducing energy cost by more than 50\% compared with a cloud-only, an
edge-only and another edge--cloud scheduler. The quality of the answers plays no part in
the decision, as the authors acknowledge, and each request is placed once, with no
second chance at a larger model.

CR$^2$~\cite{xue2026cr2} routes queries between a small model on the user's device and
a pool of larger models on an edge server. The device decides from the query alone
whether to answer locally or defer, weighing accuracy against a cost that combines
latency and energy, and the edge then picks one of its models for each deferred query.
At the same accuracy, it reduces this cost by up to 16.9\% compared with the strongest
baseline router. Energy is modeled as each platform's profiled power times its latency,
so the cost of the edge changes with the wireless channel, but not with how busy the
edge is.

Energy-aware routing has also been applied within a single system, where GreenServ
learns online which of 16 LLMs gives the best trade-off between accuracy and measured GPU
energy for each kind of query, and uses 31\% less energy than routing at
random~\cite{ziller2026greenserv}. EcoThink sends simple queries to a small model that
answers directly and complex ones to a larger model that reasons step by step, using
40.4\% less energy than reasoning on every query~\cite{li2026ecothink}.
\citeonline{wilkins2024offline} model the energy and runtime of each LLM from the lengths
of its prompts and answers, and assign a workload's queries offline among Llama-2 models
of 7, 13 and 70 billion parameters, with a parameter that trades energy for accuracy.
All three choose among models on a single system rather than across network tiers, and
in two of them the energy of a query does not depend on the others being served, since
GreenServ serves one query at a time and the energy models of
\citeauthoronline{wilkins2024offline} are measured at a fixed batch size. The latter also score each
model by a single accuracy figure, so the answer to a particular query plays no part in
their decision.

In the tiered works, the decision is made at every tier from the confidence of an
answer but leaves energy out, while the energy-aware works use energy but decide once
per query, over two tiers at most, before any model has answered.

\section{Challenges Addressed in This Work}
\label{sec:challenges}

The works reviewed above leave four gaps, and each contribution listed in
Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction} addresses one of them.

The first is that energy has no part in recursive tiered inference, since in RecServe
and in the architecture of \citeonline{pakpahan2026continuum} every tier decides from the
confidence of its answer alone. PerLLM and CR$^2$ do bring energy into the decision, but
over two tiers and with a single decision per query, made before any model has
answered~\cite{yang2024perllm,xue2026cr2}. In this work, each tier keeps a confidence
test and also weighs the energy it expects to be spent until the query is answered,
both when a query arrives and when an answer escalates, so a query can skip a tier whose answer is likely to cost more than it saves.

The second is that the energy of the tiers is not measured under a common boundary,
since the figures available for a phone, an edge board and a GPU server each count a
different part of the system, and estimates for similar tasks can be an order of
magnitude apart~\cite{elsworth2025measuring}. The energy of a server also depends on the
queries it batches together, a dependence that CR$^2$ leaves out by modeling energy as power times
latency, and neither GreenServ nor the models of \citeonline{wilkins2024offline} let it
vary with the load (Section~\ref{sec:energy-routing}). We build an energy model of the
user device, the ONU and the OLT under a single boundary, in which the OLT's energy is
measured by us on a GPU as a function of batch size and checked against the same GPU
serving with continuous batching.

The third is that the cost of the OLT changes with its load, and the tiers below cannot
observe it. One more query joining a busy batch costs far less than a query that finds
the OLT idle, so the same decision can save or waste energy depending on the moment. A
timetable learned in advance follows the daily pattern of the load but not the moments
that depart from it, so we use the PON downstream broadcast (Section~\ref{sec:pon}) to
let the OLT announce its current cost to every ONU at once, with no extra upstream traffic.

The fourth is how such policies are compared. RecServe is evaluated on the trade-off
between accuracy and communication~\cite{wu2025recserve}, and the network-side work of
\citeonline{pakpahan2026continuum} on latency, so neither reports the energy a tiered
system spends to reach a given accuracy. A policy that sends more queries to larger
models is also more accurate, so comparing policies at the same $\beta$ would mix the two
effects. We compare every policy at the same target accuracy, under recorded
conversation traffic with and without departures from its daily pattern, against
escalation by confidence alone, a timetable and each tier answering every query alone.

\section{Overview of the Case Study}
\label{sec:overview}

The case study places a three-tier hierarchy on a PON, formed by the device on which the
query is typed, the ONU in the home and the OLT at the operator's central office, which
is shared by every home on the PON. As in earlier tiered
architectures~\cite{wu2025recserve,pakpahan2026continuum}, each tier runs a larger model
than the one below it, and Table~\ref{tab:tiers} lists the three. The user tier is a
phone running a model of about one billion parameters, and the ONU is a single-board
computer with a neural accelerator, the class of device that an AI-capable home gateway
could carry. The energy of these two tiers comes from published
measurements~\cite{cai2026npu,renney2026cloud}, while that of the OLT was measured by us
on a GPU (Section~\ref{sec:energy-model}).

The hierarchy stops at the OLT, without the cloud tier that those architectures place
above it, for two reasons. The strategies compared in this work differ in how the tiers
learn the OLT's current cost, and the broadcast that carries this cost exists only in the
access network, so the access network is where they can be compared. A cloud tier was
also beyond the resources of this work, since it would have required running a model of
more than 70 billion parameters on every test problem, recording the probabilities of its
tokens, and measuring its energy against batch size on a cluster of
GPUs~\cite{pakpahan2026continuum}. The OLT is therefore the top tier, and since no tier
above it can correct its answers, its accuracy of 91.7\% is the highest any policy can
deliver. The decision rule itself does not depend on the number of tiers
(Section~\ref{sec:policy}), so a cloud tier would only add one more term to it.

The task is GSM8K, a set of grade-school math problems whose answer is a single
number~\cite{cobbe2021training}, so each answer can be graded exactly by comparing its
final number with the reference, without a judge model. The problems take between two
and eight steps to solve, which spreads the accuracies of the three models far apart.
Every tier answered each of the 1,319 test problems once, and the simulation replays
these recorded answers, together with the confidence computed from the probabilities of
their tokens (Section~\ref{sec:llm-background}), instead of running the models again.

\section{Energy-Aware Recursive Offloading}
\label{sec:policy}

In the policy we propose, a query starts at the user device, and each tier that runs it
applies the quantile test of \citeonline{wu2025recserve}
(Section~\ref{sec:llm-background}), returning its answer to the user if the answer's
confidence reaches the $\beta$-quantile of the confidences the tier has seen recently and
escalating the query otherwise. On top of this recursion, the policy chooses which tier
runs the query from the energy each tier is expected to spend. Since it never judges
whether an answer is good enough, the accuracy delivered still comes only from the models
and from the quantile test.

The choice rests on $C_j$, the expected energy spent from the moment a query is handed
to tier $j$ until some tier returns an answer. Tier $j$ always runs the query, and with
probability $p_j$ its answer fails the test and the query moves on to tier $j+1$, so
\begin{equation}
  C_j = E_j + p_j \, C_{j+1}, \qquad C_J = E_J,
  \label{eq:cost-to-completion}
\end{equation}
where $J$ is the top tier and $E_j$ is the energy tier $j$ is expected to spend running
the query. We call a tier's energy per prompt token and per generated token its energy
rates, and $E_j$ is the first rate times the length of the prompt plus the second times
the average length of the tier's past answers, which stands in for the length of this
answer because that length is only known after the tier runs.
Equation~\eqref{eq:cost-to-completion} also assumes that, after tier $j$, the query
climbs one tier at a time, as it does when confidence alone decides.

The quantile test escalates about a fraction $\beta$ of a tier's answers by construction
(Section~\ref{sec:llm-background}), so the policy sets $p_j = \beta$. The policy does not
depend on this test, though, because Equation~\eqref{eq:cost-to-completion} needs only
the probability that a tier escalates, and so it works with any escalation test, such as
a fixed threshold or a separate scoring model~\cite{chen2024frugalgpt}. A test whose rate
is not fixed in advance would need $p_j$ to be learned from the answers instead, which is
why Equation~\eqref{eq:cost-to-completion} keeps $p_j$ apart from $\beta$.

The policy uses $C_j$ at two points of Algorithm~\ref{alg:decision}. The first is when a
query arrives at a tier $i < J$ (lines 1 to 4), where running the query costs $E_i$ and,
if its answer escalates, the expected energy of the cheapest tier above. The tier
therefore forwards the query, without running it, to the tier $k^*$ above it with the
lowest $C_k$ if
\begin{equation}
  C_{k^*} < (1-\delta)\left(E_i + p_i \, C_{k^*}\right),
  \label{eq:on-arrival}
\end{equation}
and runs it otherwise. The second is when an answer escalates (lines 9 to 11), and the
query then goes to tier $i+1$, or skips directly to $k^*$ if
$C_{k^*} < (1-\delta)\,C_{i+1}$. The top tier has nowhere to send a query and always
returns its answer.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
  \caption{The decision of tier $i < J$ on a query $q$}
  \label{alg:decision}
  \KwIn{the query $q$; the quantile $\beta$; the window $W_i$ of tier $i$'s recent
    confidences; the learned averages; the energy rates of every tier above}
  \If{tier $i$ can decide}{
    $k^* \leftarrow \arg\min_{k>i} C_k$ \tcp*{Equation~\eqref{eq:cost-to-completion}}
    \If{$C_{k^*} < (1-\delta)\,(E_i + p_i\,C_{k^*})$}{
      forward $q$ to tier $k^*$ and stop\;
    }
  }
  run $q$, giving the answer $a$ with confidence $s$\;
  $\theta \leftarrow$ the $\beta$-quantile of $W_i$, then add $s$ to $W_i$\;
  \If{$s \geq \theta$}{
    return $a$ to the user and stop\;
  }
  $\mathit{next} \leftarrow i+1$\;
  \If{tier $i$ can decide \textbf{and} $C_{k^*} < (1-\delta)\,C_{i+1}$}{
    $\mathit{next} \leftarrow k^*$\;
  }
  pass $q$ to tier $\mathit{next}$\;
\end{algorithm}

The margin $\delta$ keeps a tier from moving a query for a negligible saving, and it also
sets how far the policy departs from escalation by confidence alone. With $\delta = 0$, a
tier takes any route that is expected to cost less. With $\delta = 1$, neither
inequality can hold, since every energy is positive, so each tier runs every query that
reaches it and passes on only those whose answers fail the test, which is confidence-only
escalation exactly. The main results use $\delta = 0$, and Chapter~\ref{chap:results}
sweeps the values in between.

With $\delta = 0$ and $p_i = \beta$, Equation~\eqref{eq:on-arrival} becomes
$(1-\beta)\,C_{k^*} < E_i$, or $C_{k^*} < E_i / (1-\beta)$. Running the query costs
$E_i$, and it avoids the cost $C_{k^*}$ of the tiers above only when the answer passes
the test, which happens with probability $1-\beta$. A tier therefore runs a query only
if the cheapest tier above it costs at least $E_i/(1-\beta)$. This threshold rises with
$\beta$, because the more often a tier escalates, the more often the energy it spends
running a query is wasted.

To compute $C_k$, a tier also needs the average answer length of every tier above it.
Each tier ships with the mean answer length of its model, measured on the benchmark
before deployment, and then keeps an average of the lengths of its recent answers,
weighted toward the latest so that it follows about its last 20 answers. Since an answer
travels back to the user through every tier below the one that produced it, it can
carry, at no extra cost, the energy rates and the average answer length of every tier
that ran the query, and each tier below replaces the answer lengths it holds with the
ones it hears. The OLT's average covers every household on the PON, while those of the
user device and the ONU cover only their own household, so households with different models would each
hold their own values for these two tiers. Until a tier has an estimate of the OLT's
energy rate, it runs every query that reaches it and escalates by confidence alone. For
the quantile test, each device also keeps its own window of its last 1,000 confidences,
within the 300 to 1,000 that \citeonline{wu2025recserve} find sufficient, started from
the confidences of its model on the benchmark so that the threshold is right from the
first query.

The energy rates of the user device and the ONU do not change, so the rates a tier hears
on past answers stay valid. The OLT's rate, however, depends on how many other queries
the OLT is serving at the moment (Section~\ref{sec:energy-model}), and a household hears
it on its own answers only a few times a day, so we compare two strategies for giving
every tier a current estimate of it.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Timetable.} One OLT rate per hour of the day, for weekdays and for
  weekends, learned over a month of calibration and shipped to every device in advance.
  Since the policy changes the load the timetable describes, the timetable is learned
  while the policy runs with it, and it is relearned three times, each time from the load
  the previous version produced, so that it settles.
  \item \textbf{Broadcast.} Every 10 seconds, the OLT sends downstream the energy one
  more query would add to its consumption, averaged over its last 5 minutes
  (Section~\ref{sec:accounting}). A single message reaches every ONU on the PON, and each
  ONU passes it on to the devices in its home.
\end{itemize}

\section{Energy Model}
\label{sec:energy-model}

The energy of every tier is counted at the same boundary, everything the system draws to
answer a query, as in Google's accounting of a prompt~\cite{elsworth2025measuring}. For
the user device and the ONU this is the whole device, and for the OLT it is the GPU
together with the rest of its server, the spare machines kept ready for peaks and the
building that houses them. The energy of carrying queries and answers over the PON and
the home network is left out, as are relaying the OLT's broadcast inside the home and
manufacturing the hardware. Since the sources of the three tiers count different parts
of their systems (Section~\ref{sec:measurements}), Table~\ref{tab:energy} lists what each
one measures and how it is brought to this boundary, together with the energy each tier

\subsection{User Device and ONU}
\label{sec:user-onu-energy}

The energy of the user device comes from the phone that \citeonline{cai2026npu} measured
with Qualcomm's power telemetry, running Llama-3.2-1B on its CPU, and the phone spends
0.016~J per prompt token and 0.074~J per generated token, or 15.7~J for a query of the mean
lengths. The telemetry covers the whole chip but not the memory, the display or the
radio, so this figure is a lower bound. Since escalation by confidence alone runs every
query on the user device while the policy often skips it, a device that spends more than
this figure would add more energy to the baseline than to the policy, so the lower bound
can only understate the saving.

The energy of the ONU comes from the Raspberry Pi 5 with a Hailo-10H accelerator that
\citeonline{renney2026cloud} measured with a meter at the power input, running
Qwen2.5-1.5B, and the board spends 0.88~J per generated token at 6.34 tokens per second,
or 243~J for an answer of the mean length. This figure includes the board's idle draw of
about 3.5~W~\cite{tummalapalli2026edge}, without which the ONU spends 0.33~J per token.
\citeonline{tummalapalli2026edge} measured the same model on the same hardware at 0.27~J
per token above idle, so the two sources agree. \citeonline{renney2026cloud} give
a single figure per generated token, measured with a prompt of about 12 tokens, so the
prefill of that prompt is already included in it and our energy model charges the ONU
nothing more for a longer prompt. This underestimates the energy of long prompts at the
ONU, and the follow-ups that carry their conversation are the longest prompts in the
simulation.

\subsection{OLT}
\label{sec:olt-energy}

We measured the energy of the OLT ourselves, running its model, Qwen2.5-7B in 8-bit, on
one NVIDIA L4 GPU served with vLLM 0.21.0~\cite{kwon2023vllm}, under the NVIDIA driver
580.95.05. The GPU keeps a running total of the energy it has used, which NVML reports,
so we read this total before and after each run and took the difference. This is more
reliable than sampling the power, which misses part of the runtime~\cite{yang2023part}.

We gave the GPU batches of 1 to 64 queries at once, each query generating 300 tokens.
To separate the two phases of inference (Section~\ref{sec:llm-background}), each batch
was also run generating a single token, which measures the prefill alone, so that the
decode is the difference between the two runs. Every measurement was repeated five times
after one warm-up run, and we kept the median. We also measured the GPU at rest, at
30.5~W with the model loaded, so that the energy a query adds above this idle power could
be computed.

Batching makes each query much cheaper, because the GPU draws about 72~W whatever the
batch size, while the number of tokens it generates per second grows almost in
proportion to the batch. The energy per generated token therefore falls by a factor of
51, from 2.47~J for a query alone to 0.048~J in a batch of 64, while the energy per
prompt token falls until a batch of about 12 and then stays near 0.015~J.

In these batches every query starts and ends together, while the OLT in the simulation
serves with continuous batching, where queries join and leave at any moment
(Section~\ref{sec:measurements}). The simulator we built for the case study
(Section~\ref{sec:simulation}) runs no model, and instead follows how many queries the
OLT is serving at each moment, as continuous batching does, charging each moment the
energy per token measured for that batch size. To check that this combination is valid,
we set a criterion before collecting data, under which the simulator had to predict the
energy and the mean latency of real runs with continuous batching within 10\%. We first
ran the GPU with a fixed number of requests in flight, from 1 to 64, and its energy per
token was within 3.2\% of the fixed batches (Figure~\ref{fig:batching}a). We then sent
the GPU 15 runs of requests arriving at random times and gave the same arrival times to
the simulator. A first version of the simulator, which modeled the energy of a busy GPU
as a straight line in the batch size, overestimated its energy above idle by up to 14\%
at the highest load and failed the criterion. The current version reads the measured
values at each batch size instead, and it predicts the GPU's energy within 3\% and its
mean latency within 2\% under both ways of charging energy described in
Section~\ref{sec:accounting} (Figure~\ref{fig:batching}b).

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \caption{The OLT's energy under static and continuous batching}
  \label{fig:batching}
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/batching_validation.pdf}
  \legend{Source: The author}
\end{figure}

The GPU is only part of what the OLT's server consumes. In Google's measurements, the
accelerators account for 58\% of a prompt's energy, the server's CPU and memory for 25\%,
the spare machines kept ready for peaks for 10\% and the building for
8\%~\cite{elsworth2025measuring}. Counting the first three, a server therefore spends
$(58+25+10)/58 = 1.60$ times the energy of its accelerators. In place of Google's own
building overhead, we use the industry's average PUE of 1.54~\cite{uptime2025survey},
which makes the OLT's energy the GPU's multiplied by $1.60 \times 1.54 = 2.47$. Since a
central office server with a single 72~W GPU probably spends a larger share on the rest
of the server than Google's large machines do, this figure is more likely too low than
too high.

\subsection{Average and Marginal Accounting}
\label{sec:accounting}

Since the OLT batches, the energy of a query depends on how it is charged
(Section~\ref{sec:measurements}). Under average accounting, a query pays its share of its
batch's energy, read off the sweep at the batch's size, while under marginal accounting
nothing is charged for keeping the hardware on, and a batch's energy above idle,
measured at its size, is shared equally among its queries. A query alone on the OLT
therefore costs 1,541~J under average accounting and 793~J under marginal accounting, and
one in a batch of 64 costs 34~J and 18~J respectively.

Marginal accounting also changes the other figures. Google's share for spare machines is
no longer charged, which lowers the OLT's conversion from 2.47 to 2.20, and the ONU's
idle draw is removed, which lowers its energy from 243~J to 91~J, while the phone is
charged the same under both. The study uses marginal accounting, because a routing
decision changes only what the query adds, and repeats its main runs under average
accounting as a sensitivity analysis.

The cost the OLT reports follows the same accounting. The OLT sends it on its broadcast
and also stamps it on the answers it returns, but under the broadcast strategy the tiers
take the OLT's cost only from the broadcast. A household relying on its own answers
alone would hear this cost only a few times a day, and Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} tests
this variant to show what the broadcast adds. Under marginal accounting it is what one more query would add, about 9~J
while the OLT is busy and the cost of a query alone while it is idle. The OLT reports the
average of the two, weighted by the share of its last 5 minutes it spent busy. The 9~J is
a single slope fitted over batches 1 to 64, because the energy measured between adjacent
batch sizes varies too much at small batches, from 0.4~J to 7.1~J at the GPU, and stays
between 3.5 and 4.9~J above four queries. A single value also spares the tiers from
predicting the batch their query will meet. The slope is fitted on the GPU's total energy,
which gives about 9~J instead of the 5~J of its energy above idle and so, if anything,
makes the policy look slightly worse than it is. Under average accounting, the OLT
instead reports the mean cost per token of its work over the same 5 minutes, and in
either case the timetable holds the same quantity for each hour of a weekday and of a
weekend day.

\section{Simulation}
\label{sec:simulation}

The simulator we built for the case study follows one PON through time, from the moment
each household sends a message until some tier returns its answer. It runs no model,
since every answer is the one recorded for its question (Section~\ref{sec:overview}), and
it charges each tier the energy of Section~\ref{sec:energy-model}, so what it reproduces
is when the queries arrive, where each policy sends them and how busy the OLT is when
they get there. It keeps the messages waiting to be sent in time order and takes them
one at a time. Before each message, it brings the OLT up to that moment, admitting the
queries that have reached it, finishing the answers it completes and, under the broadcast
strategy, sending the broadcast every 10 seconds. The message then climbs from the user
device as Algorithm~\ref{alg:decision} decides, and a query that leaves for the OLT joins
it after the seconds the tiers below spent on it. When an answer reaches the user, the
next message of its conversation, if there is one, is queued a pause later. The code of
the simulator and of the measurements is
public\footnote{\url{https://github.com/diegohommer/tcc-offloading-ai-edge}}. Each run
covers a test month of 28 days, preceded by a calibration month
of the same length in which the timetable is learned, and every run is repeated with
three random seeds, which change the households' conversations and the questions they
ask.

\subsection{Households and Their Traffic}
\label{sec:traffic}

Each run places between 1,000 and 20,000 households on the PON. We assume that a
household has, on average, one person who uses an LLM every week, because we found no
measured figure for Brazilian homes, and the study repeats its runs with two such people
to see how much this choice matters. That person sends 3.6 messages a day, the average
of ChatGPT's users in July 2025, when more than 700 million people used it every week
and together sent more than 2.5 billion messages a day~\cite{chatterji2025how}. Some
people chat far more than others, and in WildChat, the logs of a free ChatGPT service
used around the world~\cite{zhao2024wildchat}, we found that the busiest 10\% of the
users who came back over two weeks or more send 59\% of the messages. Each user in the simulation is therefore given
a random level of activity, chosen so that the busiest 10\% send the same share while
the average stays at 3.6 messages a day.

People send their messages in conversations, a few questions a couple of minutes apart,
and the simulation copies from WildChat when conversations start, how many messages
they hold and how long people pause between them. WildChat records its times on the
server's clock, and its users are spread over the world, so each user's times are first
moved to their own time zone. Every simulated household then shares that one local day,
as the homes on one OLT share a time zone. A conversation is a run of messages less than 10 minutes apart, and it holds
3.2 messages on average. Conversations start about seven times more often at the busiest
hour of a weekday, in the afternoon, than at the quietest, before dawn, and a weekend day
has three quarters of the conversations of a weekday. Users who sent a single message
are left out, because WildChat's sudden traffic spikes are made mostly of them, which
suggests automated traffic rather than people, and so are the messages whose time zone
cannot be told. Every message asks a question drawn at random from GSM8K, and a follow-up is sent
a pause after the previous answer arrives, with a prompt that carries the earlier turns
of its conversation, so prompts grow longer as a conversation goes on.

\subsection{Drift in the Load}
\label{sec:drift}

If every day followed the average day, a timetable would predict the OLT's load almost
perfectly, since only the randomness of the arrivals would move an hour away from it.
Real load drifts, with hours that run busier or quieter than usual, so the study has
three scenarios, of which Figure~\ref{fig:traffic-week} shows one test week. With no
drift every day follows the average day, while with mild and strong drift the load of
all households is multiplied by a common busyness factor that changes slowly over time.
The factor's mean is one, so the month carries the same number of messages, but an hour
can run, for example, a quarter busier than the timetable says and stay so for some
hours before returning to it.

Both scenarios follow the way the arrivals of people's requests are usually modeled. In
wide-area networks, packets arrive in bursts at every time scale, but the sessions that
users start, such as remote logins and file transfers, are well modeled as a Poisson
process whose rate is fixed within each hour~\cite{paxson1995wide}. The conversations of
our households are sessions of this kind, so with no drift they are drawn from a Poisson
process that follows the timetable. The rate of the same hour still changes from one day
to the next, and in call centers, whose arrivals have been studied most closely, the
count of a period varies more than a Poisson process with a fixed timetable allows and
busy periods persist. The usual model there multiplies the timetable by a random
busyness factor whose mean is one and draws the arrivals from a Poisson process at that
rate~\cite{ibrahim2012modeling}. The factor can be one for the whole day or change from
one period to the next while depending on the periods before it, and ours is of the
second kind. Our measurement below finds the same excess over Poisson counts in
BurstGPT's conversations, whose arrivals also grow more or less bursty within
twenty-minute windows during working hours~\cite{wang2025burstgpt}. In our simulation the factor $F_k$
changes every hour $k$ and is shared by the whole PON, which makes the arrivals a
log-Gaussian Cox process~\cite{moller1998log}, and it is built from a number $z_k$ that
keeps part of the previous hour's departure and receives a small random kick each hour,
as
\begin{equation}
  z_k = \phi\, z_{k-1} + \varepsilon_k, \qquad F_k = e^{\,z_k - \sigma^2/2},
  \label{eq:drift}
\end{equation}
where $\phi = e^{-1/\tau}$ is the share of the departure that the next hour keeps,
$\varepsilon_k$ is the random kick, drawn from a normal distribution with a mean of zero,
$\sigma$ sets how large a typical departure is and $\tau$, in hours, how long it lasts.
The exponential turns $z_k$ into a multiplier, so that equal steps up and down in $z_k$
make the load equally many times higher or lower, and subtracting $\sigma^2/2$ keeps the
mean of $F_k$ at one. With the values of the mild drift given below, for example,
$\phi = 0.73$, so an hour with $z_k = 0.30$, whose load is 31\% above the timetable, is
followed by hours 21\% and then 14\% above it if no new kick arrives.

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \caption{Messages per hour over one test week of 10,000 households, with no, mild and
  strong drift}
  \label{fig:traffic-week}
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/traffic_week.pdf}
  \legend{Source: The author}
\end{figure}

We measured $\sigma$ and $\tau$ on BurstGPT, whose more than 3,000 users reached the
models through one regional provider of the kind that serves companies and
campuses~\cite{wang2025burstgpt}. These users share one provider, which brings them
closer to the users of a single OLT than WildChat's, who are spread over the world and do
not share one access network. They reach the models from work or study rather than from
home, though, so their load may drift differently from that of a household. We used the
conversation log of one file of BurstGPT's second release, which spans 110 of the 213
days the dataset covers, and compared the number of conversations that start in each
hour with its timetable, the mean count of that hour over the days
of the same type, weekday or weekend. The size $\sigma$ comes from how far the hours
depart from the timetable once the randomness of counting alone is subtracted, since a
Poisson count with a random rate varies by the variance of its rate plus its
mean~\cite{cox1955some}, and only the hours with at least 20 conversations expected are
used, because in quieter hours the departure is mostly counting noise. The duration
$\tau$ comes from how closely the departures of consecutive hours follow each other, and
the code that computes both is part of the public repository.

For the mild drift, each day's timetable is first multiplied by the level of its week,
the mean ratio between the day's count and its timetable over the seven days around it,
so that a week that runs high or low as a whole counts as part of the timetable and not
as drift. This gives $\sigma = 0.235$ and $\tau = 3.2$ hours, so a typical hour runs about
26\% busier or 21\% quieter than its timetable, since $e^{0.235} = 1.26$, and such a
departure lasts about three hours. Without the weekly level, $\sigma = 0.457$ and
$\tau = 12.3$ hours, so a typical hour departs by 58\% or 37\% for about 12 hours, which
gives the strong drift. Most of this larger departure comes from BurstGPT's traffic
growing over those 110 days, which the simulation turns into random swings of about 12
hours, so the strong drift is best read as a demanding case. The calibration month
drifts as well, so the timetable learns an average that includes the drift.

\subsection{Serving the Queries}
\label{sec:serving}

The user device and the ONU answer at the speeds their sources measured. The phone reads
the prompt at 288 tokens per second and generates 72 tokens per second, while the ONU
generates 6.3 tokens per second and so takes about 44 seconds for an answer of the mean
length. The ONU's source gives no speed for reading the prompt, so, as with its energy
(Section~\ref{sec:user-onu-energy}), a longer prompt takes it no longer. Each household's
ONU answers one query at a time, in the order they were sent,
so a second query from the same home waits for the first. The OLT serves with continuous
batching (Section~\ref{sec:olt-energy}), with 64 slots, the largest batch measured, and a
query that finds every slot busy waits for one. A query reaches the OLT only after the
seconds the tiers below spent on it, so the batch it meets is the one actually running
at that moment. Its prefill is charged at the size of the batch it joins but is taken to
take no time, since it is short next to the decode, and the time spent crossing the
network, a few milliseconds, is also left out.

\subsection{What the Simulation Leaves Out}
\label{sec:simulation-limits}

The simulation reproduces how the OLT's cost changes over time, following the load of
the households and its drift, and holds the other quantities the policy reads still. The
user device and the ONU spend a fixed energy per token, while a real phone or gateway
spends more or less depending on its temperature, its battery and whatever else it is
running. Every household has the same three models and asks questions drawn at random
from the same benchmark, so the answer lengths each tier publishes barely move, while
real households would differ in their models and in what they ask. A follow-up's answer
is also the one recorded for its question alone, even though its prompt carries the
conversation.

WildChat's users chose to try a free chatbot, and a user there is an address and a
browser rather than a household, so they may not chat as a typical household does. Its
pauses also run from one answer to the next, so they include the time the next answer
took. The policy uses one mean answer length per model, although in our runs on GSM8K the
standard deviation of a single answer's length is 30 to 40\% of that mean, and the length could be predicted
from the query itself~\cite{zheng2023response}. The results therefore show what
the policy does when the main uncertainty it faces is the OLT's load.
