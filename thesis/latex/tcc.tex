\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}
%==============================================================================

As the use of Large Language Models (LLMs) becomes widespread, both at work and at
home~\cite{bick2026rapid}, the challenge of adapting the network architecture to handle 
this rapidly evolving technology remains an active research
topic~\cite{chen2026network}. Since evolution seldom happens in isolation, the network
itself has also been undergoing changes, one of which is the adoption of Passive Optical
Network (PON) technology in the access network~\cite{effenberger2007introduction}. Driven
by the demand for greater capacity, lower latency, and reduced operating costs, PON is
being deployed alongside or in place of the Hybrid Fiber-Coaxial (HFC)
plant~\cite{prodnik2025migration}. Quality of service, scalability, security, and energy
cost are some of the broad topics that need to be taken into account as both of these
changes happen in parallel.

On the models' side, cost is a concern as well, since LLM inference is
expensive~\cite{chen2026network}. One way of making it cheaper is the model cascade: a
smaller model answers what it can, and the query is only passed to a larger one when
its answer is not reliable enough~\cite{chen2024frugalgpt}. What counts as expensive
depends on the setting, from the price of an API call to the energy spent answering
each query, and energy is the cost this work is concerned with. Treating it as a cost
of its own is the idea behind Green AI, which argues that efficiency should be reported alongside
accuracy~\cite{schwartz2020green}, and some serving systems already use it to decide
which model answers a query: GreenServ picks a model from a heterogeneous pool based on
the accuracy and energy it has observed, and uses 31\% less energy than routing at
random~\cite{ziller2026greenserv}.

These systems choose which model answers, but not where it runs, which is a separate
question. With edge computing, inference is no longer confined to the cloud, but spread
across end devices, edge nodes, and cloud servers, each tier further from the user than
the one below, and with more computing power~\cite{chen2026network}. RecServe puts the two ideas together, placing a cascade on
the tiers of such a network and letting each tier decide, from the confidence scores it
has seen recently, whether to answer the query or to pass it up~\cite{wu2025recserve}.
Following RecServe's decision rule, \citeonline{pakpahan2026continuum} bring tiered
LLM inference into the access network itself, equipping Optical Network Units (ONUs)
and Optical Line Terminals (OLTs) to run part of the inference, so that a Time and
Wavelength Division Multiplexed PON (TWDM-PON) provides the edge and fog tiers of
such a hierarchy. In both works, however, a tier decides whether to pass a query up
from confidence alone, and the energy spent at each tier plays no part in that
decision.

In this work, we propose the integration of energy awareness into recursive tiered LLM
inference.
Building on RecServe's recursive offloading, a tier decides whether to answer a query or
pass it up based not only on its confidence, but also on the energy spent answering at
each tier. As a case study, we simulate a three-tier
hierarchy over a PON, formed by the user device, the ONU and the OLT, each running a
larger model than the one below. We evaluate two energy-aware strategies: one follows
a fixed timetable of the OLT's cost, while the other takes advantage of the PON
downstream broadcast, with the OLT periodically announcing its current cost to every
ONU. Both are compared with RecServe at the same target accuracy, in energy per query
and in communication burden, under recorded conversation traffic with and without
unforeseen surges. \todo{one or two sentences with the main results, once the Results
chapter is settled.}

The contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The integration of energy awareness into recursive tiered LLM inference, in which the
  decision to answer a query or pass it up takes into account the energy spent at each
  tier, and not only confidence;
  \item An energy model of the user device, the ONU and the OLT under a single
  measurement boundary, with the OLT's energy measured by us on a Graphics Processing Unit (GPU);
  \item An offloading strategy that uses the PON downstream broadcast to share the
  OLT's current cost with every ONU;
  \item A simulation study on recorded conversation traffic, with and without
  unforeseen surges, comparing the energy-aware strategies with RecServe in energy per
  query and communication burden.
\end{itemize}

\section{Passive Optical Networks}
\label{sec:pon}
A PON is an access network technology that uses a shared fiber architecture, where a
single fiber coming out of an OLT is split passively to serve several dozen ONUs at the
subscribers' premises, up to 128 of them in Gigabit-capable PON
(G-PON). The OLT downstream works as a broadcast,
transmitting a data signal that the splitters passively replicate across all the ONUs
connected to it. Each ONU looks at the frame headers and extracts only the data that is
addressed to it, discarding the rest~\cite{effenberger2007introduction}.

In the opposite direction, since the fiber is shared between multiple ONUs, the upstream
operates using Time Division Multiple Access (TDMA) to avoid collisions. The OLT assigns
strictly synchronized time slots to each ONU via Dynamic Bandwidth Allocation (DBA), so
each ONU can take turns bursting data back~\cite{effenberger2007introduction}. The two
directions are therefore not symmetric: anything the OLT sends reaches every ONU at once,
while each ONU can only speak in its own slot. Figure~\ref{fig:pon} shows both
directions.

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \caption{Downstream broadcast and upstream TDMA in a PON}
  \label{fig:pon}
  \vspace{6pt}
  \input{figures/pon_broadcast_tdma}
  \legend{Source: Adapted from \cite{roonie2013pon}}
\end{figure}

More recently, TWDM-PON, selected as the primary solution for the Next-Generation PON Stage 2 (NG-PON2), stacked several wavelength pairs on
top of the time sharing, each pair running its own TDMA, to multiply the capacity of the
same fiber~\cite{luo2013twdm}. TWDM-PON is the setting in
which \citeonline{pakpahan2026continuum} place tiered LLM inference
(Section~\ref{sec:pakpahan}), and the downstream broadcast is what one of the strategies
evaluated in this work relies on: a single message from the OLT reaches every ONU, with
no extra upstream traffic (Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}).

\section{LLM Inference and Model Cascades}
\label{sec:llm-background}

LLMs are scaled Pre-trained Language Models (PLMs): Transformer models with
self-attention mechanisms~\cite{vaswani2017attention}, pre-trained on large-scale
text corpora, that solve a wide range of tasks using natural
language. Researchers noticed that the more a PLM is scaled,
in model size or in training data, the more capable it tends to become at problem
solving. Large PLMs also show abilities that smaller ones lack, such as solving a new
task from a few examples given in the prompt~\cite{zhao2023survey}. However, the
size of these models places heavy demands on memory, computation and energy when serving
them~\cite{chen2026network}.

Services that use LLMs respond to a user query in two phases~\cite{zhou2024survey}. In the prefill phase, the LLM processes the whole user prompt at once, storing
intermediate results for every prompt token (the Key-Value, or KV, cache) and generating
the first output token. In the decode phase, it generates the rest of the answer one
token at a time, each step reusing the KV cache and adding the new token to it
(Figure~\ref{fig:prefill-decode}). The two
phases use the hardware differently: prefill processes many tokens in parallel and tends
to be limited by computation, while each decode step processes a single token but moves
about as much data as prefill, which makes it limited by memory
bandwidth~\cite{zhong2024distserve}. This is why the energy of a query
is often reported separately for each phase (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}).

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \caption{Prefill and decode phases of LLM inference}
  \label{fig:prefill-decode}
  \vspace{6pt}
  \input{figures/prefill_decode}
  \legend{Source: Adapted from \cite{zhou2024survey,zhong2024distserve}}
\end{figure}

To generate each token, the LLM assigns a probability to every token in its vocabulary,
and a low probability on the tokens it picks is a sign that it is unsure of the
answer~\cite{zhao2023survey}. A confidence score turns these probabilities into
a single number per answer. Ideally, that number is calibrated, meaning that answers
given with 80\% confidence are correct 80\% of the time, but modern neural networks are
often poorly calibrated, and tend to be more confident than they are
accurate~\cite{guo2017calibration}. For generated text, the most common scores
combine the probabilities of all the generated tokens, for example through their
average, their minimum, or the likelihood of the whole answer normalized by its
length~\cite{geng2023survey}. Perplexity is closely related to the last one:
it is the inverse probability of a sequence, normalized by its number of tokens, so the
more likely the answer, the lower its perplexity~\cite[ch.~3]{jurafsky2026speech}.
RecServe uses a normalized perplexity as the confidence of its generation
tasks~\cite{wu2025recserve}, and compares it with the confidences each tier has seen
recently, rather than with a fixed threshold that would have to be tuned by hand for each
task, model and network condition (Section~\ref{sec:recserve}).

A model cascade places several models one after the other, from the cheapest to the most
expensive. The query is sent to the cheapest one first, and if its answer is reliable,
that answer is returned and the more expensive models are never queried; otherwise, the query moves on to the next model
in the list. FrugalGPT judges reliability with a separate scoring function that gives
each answer a score between 0 and 1, and with it matches the performance of GPT-4 at up
to 98\% lower cost~\cite{chen2024frugalgpt}. Cascades also differ in how that
judgement is made. In AutoMix, the smaller model checks its own answer through few-shot
self-verification, without extensive training, and since this check can be noisy, a
router decides from the resulting confidence whether to move up to a larger model,
reducing the computational cost by over 50\% at comparable
performance~\cite{aggarwal2023automix}.

\section{Tiered LLM Inference}
\label{sec:tiered}

The idea of a model cascade was also brought into the network itself, by organizing it
in tiers that go from the user's own device, through the edge and the fog, up to the
cloud, with a larger, more capable LLM at each step up~\cite{pakpahan2026continuum}. A
query then climbs the tiers only as far as it needs, so simpler queries are answered
before they reach the cloud. Answering closer to the user shortens the network path and
lowers latency~\cite{chen2026network}, and fewer queries crossing the network means less
traffic: RecServe, for instance, reduces the communication burden by over 50\% compared
with serving every query in the cloud~\cite{wu2025recserve}.
