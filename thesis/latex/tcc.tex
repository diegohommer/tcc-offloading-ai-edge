\begin{abstract}
Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly served across the tiers of a network, from the
user's device to servers at its edge and in the cloud. In recursive tiered inference, a query climbs these tiers
one at a time, and each one either answers it or passes it up. Current approaches make this
choice from the confidence of the answer alone, ignoring the energy each tier spends, even
though a server that batches queries can answer one more query at little extra cost while it is
busy. This
work integrates energy awareness into recursive tiered
inference through WattLadder, a policy in which each tier also weighs whether answering a query
itself or further up would cost less energy. We study it on a Passive Optical Network (PON), with the user's
phone, the Optical Network Unit (ONU) and the Optical Line Terminal (OLT) as the three tiers and
the OLT's energy measured by us on a GPU. The tiers learn the OLT's cost either from a timetable
built in advance or from a live report that the OLT broadcasts on the PON downstream. In
simulations driven by
recorded conversation traffic, with and without drift in the load, WattLadder reaches the same
accuracy as escalation by confidence alone with less energy and a shorter wait for an answer.
The saving appears once the OLT serves enough households to batch their queries, reaching about
half the energy at 20,000 households. The timetable saves more in most scenarios, while the
broadcast saves more when changes in the load move the OLT between batching and idling.
\end{abstract}

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}
%==============================================================================

As the use of Large Language Models (LLMs) becomes widespread, both at work and at
home~\cite{bick2026rapid}, the challenge of adapting the network architecture to serve
this rapidly evolving technology closer to the user remains an active research
topic~\cite{chen2026network}. The part of the network closest to the user, the access
network that connects homes to the operator's central office, has itself been undergoing
changes, one of which is the adoption of Passive Optical Network (PON)
technology~\cite{effenberger2007introduction}. Driven by the demand for greater capacity,
lower latency, and reduced operating costs, PON is being deployed alongside or in place
of the Hybrid Fiber-Coaxial (HFC) plant~\cite{prodnik2025migration}. Quality of service,
scalability, security, and energy cost are some of the broad topics that need to be taken
into account as these two changes meet in the access network.

On the models' side, cost is a concern as well, since LLM inference is
expensive~\cite{chen2026network}. One way of making it cheaper is the model cascade,
in which a smaller model answers what it can and passes a query to a larger one only when
its answer is not reliable enough~\cite{chen2024frugalgpt}. What counts as expensive
depends on the setting, from the price of an API call to the energy spent answering
each query, and energy is the cost this work is concerned with. Treating it as a cost
of its own is the idea behind Green AI, which argues that efficiency should be reported alongside
accuracy~\cite{schwartz2020green}, and some serving systems already consider energy when
deciding which model answers a query. GreenServ, for instance, picks a model from a heterogeneous
pool based on the accuracy and energy it has observed, and uses 31\% less energy than
routing at random~\cite{ziller2026greenserv}.

These systems choose which model answers, but not where it runs, which is a separate
question. With edge computing, inference is no longer confined to the cloud, but spread
across end devices, edge nodes, and cloud servers, each tier further from the user than
the one below, and with more computing power~\cite{chen2026network}. RecServe puts the two ideas together, placing a cascade on
the tiers of such a network and letting each tier decide, from the confidence scores it
has seen recently, whether to answer the query or to pass it up~\cite{wu2025recserve}.
Following RecServe's decision rule, \citeonline{pakpahan2026continuum} bring tiered
LLM inference into the access network itself, equipping Optical Network Units (ONUs)
and Optical Line Terminals (OLTs) to run part of the inference, so that a Time and
Wavelength Division Multiplexed PON (TWDM-PON) provides the edge and fog tiers of
such a hierarchy. In both works, however, a tier decides whether to pass a query up
from confidence alone, and the energy spent at each tier plays no part in that
decision.

In this work, we propose the integration of energy awareness into recursive tiered LLM
inference, so that each tier decides whether to answer a query or pass it up from the
confidence of its answer and also from the energy expected to be spent at each tier, and
we call the resulting policy WattLadder. As a case study, we simulate a three-tier
hierarchy over a PON-based access network: the user device, the ONU and the OLT, each running a
larger model than the one below. We evaluate WattLadder with two strategies for learning the
OLT's cost: one that
follows a fixed timetable of the OLT's cost and one that takes advantage of the PON
downstream broadcast, with the OLT periodically announcing its current cost to every
ONU. Both are compared with escalation by confidence alone at the same target accuracy, in energy per query,
in latency and in communication burden, under recorded conversation traffic with and without
drift in the load. In our simulations, WattLadder reaches the same accuracy as escalation by
confidence alone with less energy, about half as much at 20,000 households, a saving that grows
with the number of households the OLT serves, since more of their queries can share a batch. The timetable saves more than
the broadcast in most scenarios, while the broadcast follows changes in the load as they happen
and needs no training.

The contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The integration of energy awareness into recursive tiered LLM inference, through
  WattLadder, a policy in which the decision to answer a query or pass it up takes into
  account the energy spent at each tier, and not only confidence;
  \item An energy model of the user device, the ONU and the OLT counted toward a single
  measurement boundary, with the OLT's energy measured by us on a Graphics Processing Unit (GPU);
  \item Two strategies for sharing the OLT's cost with the tiers below, a timetable learned
  in advance and a report broadcast on the PON downstream, with the conditions under which
  each saves more;
  \item A simulation study on recorded conversation traffic, with and without
  drift in the load, comparing the energy-aware strategies with confidence-only
  escalation in energy per query, latency and communication burden.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this work is organized as follows. Chapter~\ref{chap:review}
presents the background on PONs, LLM inference and how its energy is measured, and
reviews the work on tiered LLM inference and on energy-aware LLM serving.
Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} presents the case study and WattLadder, with its two strategies
for learning the OLT's cost, then the energy model of the three tiers, the simulator and how the
policies are compared. Chapter~\ref{chap:results} compares the energy each policy needs to reach
the same accuracy, the two strategies, and the latency and traffic that come with the saving,
then covers a margin that trades energy for traffic, the sensitivity of the results and the
limitations of the study. Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} concludes the work and points to future
work.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Passive Optical Networks}
\label{sec:pon}
A PON is an access network technology that uses a shared fiber architecture, where a
single fiber coming out of one of the OLT's ports is split passively to serve several
dozen ONUs at the subscribers' premises, up to 128 of them in Gigabit-capable PON
(G-PON), and an OLT has many such ports, each serving its own PON. Because the fiber is
shared, the OLT downstream works as a broadcast, with the splitters passively
replicating its signal across all the ONUs of that PON, and each ONU looks at the
frame headers and keeps only the data addressed to it, discarding the
rest~\cite{effenberger2007introduction}.

In the opposite direction, the ONUs share the fiber through Time Division Multiple
Access (TDMA) to avoid collisions, with the OLT assigning each ONU strictly synchronized
time slots through Dynamic Bandwidth Allocation (DBA), so that the ONUs take turns
bursting data back~\cite{effenberger2007introduction}. The two directions are therefore
not symmetric: anything the OLT sends on a port reaches every ONU of that PON at once,
while each ONU can only speak in its own slot (Figure~\ref{fig:pon}).

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \caption{Downstream broadcast and upstream TDMA in a PON}
  \label{fig:pon}
  \vspace{6pt}
  \input{figures/pon_broadcast_tdma}
  \legend{Source: Adapted from \cite{roonie2013pon}}
\end{figure}

More recently, TWDM-PON, selected as the primary solution for the Next-Generation PON Stage 2 (NG-PON2), stacked several wavelength pairs on
top of the time sharing, each pair running its own TDMA, to multiply the capacity of the
same fiber~\cite{luo2013twdm}, and it is the setting in which
\citeonline{pakpahan2026continuum} place tiered LLM inference
(Section~\ref{sec:pakpahan}). One of the strategies evaluated in this work relies on
the downstream broadcast, since a single message on each of the OLT's ports reaches every
ONU behind it with no extra upstream traffic (Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}).

\subsection{LLM Inference}
\label{sec:llm-background}

LLMs are scaled Pre-trained Language Models (PLMs), Transformer models with
self-attention mechanisms~\cite{vaswani2017attention} that are pre-trained on large-scale
text corpora and solve a wide range of tasks using natural language. Researchers noticed
that the more a PLM is scaled, in model size or in training data, the more capable it
tends to become at problem solving, and large PLMs also show abilities that smaller ones
lack, such as solving a new task from a few examples given in the
prompt~\cite{zhao2023survey}. The same size, however, places heavy demands on memory,
computation and energy when these models are served~\cite{chen2026network}.

These demands come from how an LLM answers a query, which happens in two
phases. In the prefill phase, the LLM processes the whole user prompt at once, storing
intermediate results for every prompt token (the Key-Value, or KV, cache) and generating
the first output token. In the decode phase, it generates the rest of the answer one
token at a time, each step reusing the KV cache and adding the new token to it
(Figure~\ref{fig:prefill-decode}). The two phases use the hardware differently, as prefill processes many tokens in
parallel and tends to be limited by computation, while each decode step processes a single token
but moves about as much data as prefill, which makes it limited by memory
bandwidth~\cite{zhong2024distserve}. For this reason, the energy of a query is often
reported separately for each phase (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}).

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \caption{Prefill and decode phases of LLM inference}
  \label{fig:prefill-decode}
  \vspace{6pt}
  \input{figures/prefill_decode}
  \legend{Source: Adapted from \cite{zhong2024distserve}}
\end{figure}

To generate each token, the LLM assigns a probability to every token in its vocabulary,
and a low probability on the tokens it picks is a sign that it is unsure of the
answer. A confidence score turns these probabilities into
a single number per answer, and for generated text, widely used scores combine the
probabilities of all the generated tokens, for example through their average, their
minimum, or the likelihood of the whole answer normalized by its
length~\cite{geng2023survey}. Perplexity, closely related to the last one, is the
inverse probability of a sequence normalized by its number of tokens, so the more likely
the answer, the lower its perplexity~\cite[ch.~3]{jurafsky2026speech}.

Ideally, a score is calibrated, meaning that answers given with 80\% confidence are
correct 80\% of the time, but modern neural networks are often poorly calibrated and
tend to be more confident than they are accurate~\cite{guo2017calibration}. A fixed
threshold on such a score would therefore have to be tuned by hand for each task, model
and network condition, so a model can instead compare each confidence with the ones it
has seen recently. It keeps the confidences of its last $k$ answers and accepts an answer
whose confidence reaches their $\beta$-quantile, so about a fraction $\beta$ of its
answers are rejected by construction, whatever the scale of the score. This test was
proposed by \citeonline{wu2025recserve}, who turn the perplexity of generated text into a
confidence between 0 and 1 as $1/(1+\text{perplexity})$, and we refer to it as the quantile
test. We score an answer by the geometric mean of the probabilities of its tokens, which is one
over the perplexity, so it orders answers the same way and the test makes the same decisions.

\subsection{Model Cascades and Tiered Inference}
\label{sec:cascades-tiers}

A model cascade places several models one after the other, from the cheapest to the most
expensive, and sends each query to the cheapest one first. If that model's answer is
reliable, it is returned and the more expensive models are never queried, and otherwise
the query moves on to the next model in the list. FrugalGPT judges reliability with a separate scoring function that gives
each answer a score between 0 and 1, and with it matches the performance of GPT-4 at up
to 98\% lower cost~\cite{chen2024frugalgpt}. Cascades also differ in how that
judgement is made, and in AutoMix the smaller model checks its own answer through
few-shot self-verification, without extensive training. Since this check can be noisy, a router then decides from the
resulting confidence whether to move up to a larger model, reducing the computational
cost by over 50\% at comparable performance~\cite{aggarwal2023automix}.

The idea of a model cascade was also brought into the network itself, whose tiers go
from the user's own device, through the edge and the fog, up to the cloud, with a larger,
more capable LLM at each step up~\cite{pakpahan2026continuum}. A query then climbs the
tiers only as far as it needs, so simpler queries are answered before they reach the
cloud, closer to the user, where the shorter network path lowers
latency~\cite{chen2026network}. Fewer queries crossing the network also means less
traffic, and a cascade over the device, the edge and the cloud that escalates with the
quantile test reduces the communication burden by over 50\% compared with serving every
query in the cloud~\cite{wu2025recserve}.

\subsection{Measuring LLM Inference Energy}
\label{sec:metrics}
\label{sec:measurements}

Studies report the energy of LLM inference either per generated token or per
query~\cite{solovyeva2026green,elsworth2025measuring,chung2025mlenergy}, and each unit
can be further split into the prefill and decode phases~\cite{solovyeva2026green}. Of
these units, this work compares tiers per query, since different
models can answer the same prompt with responses of very different lengths, and a figure
per token would hide how verbose each model is~\cite{chung2025mlenergy}. Each query's
energy is still built from the two phases, as the energy per prompt token in the prefill
plus the energy per generated token in the decode (Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}).

What a figure means depends on its measurement boundary: the set of components whose
energy it counts. A boundary can cover only the accelerator or also the host's CPU
and memory, the idle machines kept ready for peaks, and the overhead of the building
itself. That
overhead is measured by the Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE), the ratio between a
facility's total energy and the energy of its computing equipment. In Google's
accounting of a median prompt, the accelerators take 58\% of the energy, the host's CPU
and memory 24\%, idle machines 10\% and the data center's overhead 8\%, and since there
is no consensus on which of these to count, published estimates for similar tasks can be
an order of magnitude apart~\cite{elsworth2025measuring}. GPUs account for 50--70\% of a
data center's provisioned power~\cite{chung2025mlenergy}, so measuring only the GPU
leaves out a large part of a query's energy, and figures from different studies cannot
be compared directly.

Servers usually answer several queries at once, and batching them together amortizes
fixed costs such as memory transfers, although its effect on energy depends on the
inference phase and on the setup~\cite{delavande2026understanding}. Since an LLM
generates its answer one token per step, the queries in a batch finish at different
times. Earlier serving systems kept the batch fixed until every query in it was done, so
a finished query could not return to the user and a new one had to wait. Orca instead
schedules one step at a time, so queries join and leave the batch between
steps~\cite{yu2022orca}. vLLM batches the same way and also stores the KV cache in
fixed-size blocks, so more queries fit in a batch. Its throughput is 2--4 times that of
Orca and FasterTransformer at the same latency~\cite{kwon2023vllm}. We refer to this way
of serving as continuous batching.

Because a query's energy then depends on the other queries in its batch, and because the
hardware draws power even at rest, there is more than one way to charge it. The GPU reports
only the energy of the whole batch, so each query's part has to be split out of it by some
rule. The usual rule divides the batch's energy in proportion to each query's tokens, but
\citeonline{luo2026request} measured what each query actually adds to its batch and found
that this rule overcharges some queries and undercharges others by a wide margin, under
static and continuous batching alike. Any such rule still adds up to the batch's energy, so
its error moves energy between queries without changing the total, and the energy per query
that this work reports, the total divided by the number of queries, does not depend on it.
The other choice is whether to charge the power the hardware draws at rest, which is what
separates average from marginal accounting. Average accounting charges each query its share
of everything the hardware draws while serving its batch, while marginal accounting shares
among the queries only the energy their work adds above idle
(Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}).

The boundary also depends on the tool, since each class of hardware is measured
differently. GPUs report their own energy in software, read through the NVIDIA
Management Library (NVML) on the NVIDIA GPUs this work uses, while libraries such as
Zeus, used by \citeonline{chung2025mlenergy}, read it on both NVIDIA and AMD GPUs.
These readings have limits of their own, since on the A100 and H100 the power that
nvidia-smi reports was found to sample only a quarter of the runtime, which can lead to a large
under- or overestimate of the energy spent~\cite{yang2023part}. Phones expose on-chip
telemetry that reports the energy of the whole chip~\cite{cai2026npu}, while single-board
edge devices are measured either at the plug, with a power meter that includes the whole
system~\cite{renney2026cloud}, or with current sensors on the board's power
rails~\cite{tummalapalli2026edge}. Since each tool covers a different part of the
system, Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} converts every tier of this work to a single
boundary.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Tiered LLM Inference}
\label{sec:tiered}

The works closest to ours place a cascade of LLMs on the tiers of a network.
\label{sec:recserve}
RecServe~\cite{wu2025recserve} deploys progressively larger models on the end device,
the edge node and the cloud, and every query starts at the device. Each tier keeps the
confidence scores of its last $k$ answers and sets its threshold at their
$\beta$-quantile. An answer whose confidence reaches the threshold goes back to the
user, and otherwise the query moves to the next tier, which repeats the test until the
query reaches the cloud. The threshold follows each tier's own recent scores, so it needs no
manual tuning, and each tier passes up roughly a fraction $\beta$ of its queries. On
sentiment classification and translation tasks, RecServe reaches accuracies close to
those of the cloud alone, with a better trade-off between accuracy and communication
than a cascade with fixed, hand-tuned thresholds. RecServe's theoretical
analysis considers the cost of each tier, but only as a fixed number to guide the choice
of $\beta$, and each query is still decided on its confidence alone.

\label{sec:pakpahan}
\citeonline{pakpahan2026continuum} bring this hierarchy into the access network, mapping
four tiers onto a TWDM-PON (Figure~\ref{fig:pakpahan}): tiny LLMs on user devices,
quantized models on ONUs equipped with an accelerator, models of 7 to 13 billion
parameters on OLTs at the central office, and larger models in the cloud. Most of their
work is on the network side, where a Software-Defined Networking (SDN) controller gives
inference traffic its own priority queue in the DBA and switches on a second wavelength
under congestion, reducing average inference latency by up to 50\% in simulation. Each
tier decides whether to serve a request or pass it up with RecServe's rule, so here too
the decision rests on confidence alone.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \caption{Tiered LLM inference continuum on a PON}
  \label{fig:pakpahan}
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/pakpahan2026_fig1_tiered_continuum.jpg}
  \legend{Source: \cite{pakpahan2026continuum}}
\end{figure}

The other edge--cloud methods compared by \citeauthoronline{pakpahan2026continuum} split the
work differently. Tabi~\cite{wang2023tabi} is a two-model cascade within one server,
passing a query to the larger model when the smaller one's calibrated confidence falls
below a cut-off profiled offline for each task, and with a probability that falls as the
confidence approaches one above it. EdgeShard~\cite{zhang2024edgeshard}
splits a single LLM into shards of layers placed on edge devices and a cloud server, so
every query runs the whole model and there is no decision to make per query. In the
draft--verify method of \citeonline{hao2024hybrid}, a small model at the edge generates
blocks of tokens that a large model in the cloud verifies, replacing the tokens it finds
unlikely. It reaches the quality of the large model on GSM8K at 25.8\% of its cost,
although every block needs a round trip to the cloud.

Of the works above, only RecServe and the architecture of
\citeauthoronline{pakpahan2026continuum} decide at every tier whether to answer a query
or pass it up, and both decide from confidence alone, so none of these works, those two
included, uses the energy spent at each tier to decide where a query is answered.

\subsection{Energy-Aware LLM Serving and Routing}
\label{sec:energy-routing}

Other works bring energy into the decision itself, choosing where, or by which model, a
query is answered. PerLLM~\cite{yang2024perllm} schedules LLM requests between edge servers running small
models and a cloud server running a larger one. A central scheduler assigns each request
to one server so as to minimize transmission, inference and idle energy while meeting
its deadline, reducing energy cost by more than 50\% compared with a cloud-only, an
edge-only and another edge--cloud scheduler. The quality of the answers plays no part in
the decision, as the authors acknowledge, and each request is placed once, with no
second chance at a larger model.

CR$^2$~\cite{xue2026cr2} routes queries between a small model on the user's device and
a pool of larger models on an edge server. The device decides from the query alone
whether to answer locally or defer, weighing accuracy against a cost that combines
latency and energy, and the edge then picks one of its models for each deferred query.
At the same accuracy, it reduces this cost by up to 16.9\% compared with the strongest
baseline router. Energy is modeled as each platform's profiled power times its latency,
so the cost of the edge changes with the wireless channel, but not with how busy the
edge is.

Energy-aware routing has also been applied within a single system, where GreenServ
learns online which of 16 LLMs gives the best trade-off between accuracy and measured GPU
energy for each kind of query, and uses 31\% less energy than routing at
random~\cite{ziller2026greenserv}. EcoThink sends simple queries to a small model that
answers directly and complex ones to a larger model that reasons step by step, using
40.4\% less energy than reasoning on every query~\cite{li2026ecothink}.
\citeonline{wilkins2024offline} model the energy and runtime of each LLM from the lengths
of its prompts and answers, and assign a workload's queries offline among Llama-2 models
of 7, 13 and 70 billion parameters, with a parameter that trades energy for accuracy.
All three choose among models on a single system rather than across network tiers, and
in two of them the energy of a query does not depend on the others being served, since
GreenServ serves one query at a time and the energy models of
\citeauthoronline{wilkins2024offline} are measured at a fixed batch size. The latter also score each
model by a single accuracy figure, so the answer to a particular query plays no part in
their decision.

In the tiered works, the decision is made at every tier from the confidence of an
answer but leaves energy out, while the energy-aware works use energy but decide once
per query, over two tiers at most, before any model has answered.

\section{Challenges Addressed in This Work}
\label{sec:challenges}

The works reviewed above leave four gaps, and each contribution listed in
Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction} addresses one of them.

The first is that energy has no part in recursive tiered inference, since in RecServe
and in the architecture of \citeonline{pakpahan2026continuum} every tier decides from the
confidence of its answer alone. PerLLM and CR$^2$ do bring energy into the decision, but
over two tiers and with a single decision per query, made before any model has
answered~\cite{yang2024perllm,xue2026cr2}. In WattLadder, each tier keeps a confidence
test and also weighs the energy it expects to be spent until the query is answered,
both when a query arrives and when an answer escalates, so a query can skip a tier whose answer is likely to cost more than it saves.

The second is that the energy of the tiers is not measured under a common boundary,
since the figures available for a phone, an edge board and a GPU server each count a
different part of the system, and estimates for similar tasks can be an order of
magnitude apart~\cite{elsworth2025measuring}. The energy of a server also depends on the
queries it batches together, a dependence that CR$^2$ leaves out by modeling energy as power times
latency, and neither GreenServ nor the models of \citeonline{wilkins2024offline} let it
vary with the load (Section~\ref{sec:energy-routing}). We build an energy model of the
user device, the ONU and the OLT under a single boundary, in which the OLT's energy is
measured by us on a GPU as a function of batch size and checked against the same GPU
serving with continuous batching.

The third is that the cost of the OLT changes with its load, and the tiers below cannot
observe it. One more query joining a busy batch costs far less than a query that finds
the OLT idle, so the same decision can save or waste energy depending on the moment. We
compare two ways of letting the tiers know this cost, a timetable learned in advance, which
follows the daily pattern of the load but not the moments that depart from it, and the PON
downstream broadcast (Section~\ref{sec:pon}), with which the OLT announces its current cost to
every ONU at once, with no extra upstream traffic.

The fourth is how such policies are compared. RecServe is evaluated on the trade-off
between accuracy and communication~\cite{wu2025recserve}, and the network-side work of
\citeonline{pakpahan2026continuum} on latency, so neither reports the energy a tiered
system spends to reach a given accuracy. Comparing policies at the same $\beta$ is not
enough for this, because $\beta$ fixes how often each tier escalates, not how many queries
end up at the OLT. At $\beta = 0.5$, for instance, escalation by confidence alone sends
about a quarter of the queries to the OLT, since half of them leave the phone and half of
those leave the ONU, while the same rule without the ONU sends about half, and WattLadder,
which can send a query past a tier without testing it there, may send nearly all of them
when the OLT is busy. The more queries reach the OLT, the more are answered correctly, so at
the same $\beta$ these policies differ in accuracy as well as in energy, and one could seem
to save energy only because it answers fewer queries correctly. We therefore compare every
policy at the same target accuracy, reading the energy each needs to reach it
(Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}), under recorded
conversation traffic with and without departures from its daily pattern, against
escalation by confidence alone, the same rule without the ONU and each tier answering every
query alone.

\section{Overview of the Case Study}
\label{sec:overview}

The case study places a three-tier hierarchy on a PON-based access network: the device on which the query is typed, the ONU in the home and the OLT at the operator's
central office, which serves every household connected to its PON ports and shares its model
among them. As in earlier tiered
architectures~\cite{wu2025recserve,pakpahan2026continuum}, each tier runs a larger model
than the one below it, in the size range that \citeonline{pakpahan2026continuum} assign
to it, and Table~\ref{tab:tiers} lists the three. The user tier is a
phone running a model of about one billion parameters, and the ONU is a single-board
computer with a neural accelerator, the class of device that an AI-capable home gateway
could carry. The energy of these two tiers comes from published
measurements~\cite{cai2026npu,renney2026cloud}, while that of the OLT was measured by us
on a GPU (Section~\ref{sec:energy-model}).

The hierarchy stops at the OLT, without the cloud tier that those architectures place
above it, for two reasons. The first is that this work focuses on edge inference at the PON access network,
whose tiers end at the OLT. The second is that a cloud tier was beyond the resources of
this work, since the size ranges we follow put it at more than 70 billion parameters, a
model we could not run on every test problem and measure against batch size without a
cluster of GPUs. Leaving it out does not
restrict WattLadder, whose decision rule does not depend on the number of tiers
(Section~\ref{sec:policy}), so a cloud tier would only add one more level to its
recursion. The OLT is therefore the top tier, and its accuracy of 91.7\% caps what a policy
can deliver, except on the 2.0\% of problems (26 of 1,319) that a lower tier gets right and
the OLT gets wrong.

\section{Energy-Aware Recursive Offloading}
\label{sec:policy}

In WattLadder, the policy we propose, a query starts at the user device, and each tier it reaches
first decides whether to run it or to send it straight to a cheaper one above, based on
the energy each one is expected to spend. A tier that runs the query applies the quantile
test described in Section~\ref{sec:llm-background}~\cite{wu2025recserve}, returning its
answer to the user if the answer's confidence reaches the $\beta$-quantile of the
confidences the tier has seen recently and escalating the query otherwise. The query then
goes to the next tier, or further up if that saves energy.

Both choices rest on $C_j$, the expected energy spent from the moment a query is handed
to tier $j$ until some tier returns an answer, assuming that every tier from $j$ up decides
as WattLadder does. If tier $j$ forwards the query on arrival, it sends it to
$k^*$, the tier $k$ above it with the lowest expected energy $C_k$, so the query costs
$C_{k^*}$. Otherwise tier $j$ runs
it, and with probability $p_j$ its answer fails the test and the query moves on to tier
$n$, which is $k^*$ unless the margin $\delta$ introduced below keeps it at $j+1$, so
\begin{equation}
  C_j =
  \begin{cases}
    C_{k^*} & \text{if tier } j \text{ forwards the query},\\
    E_j + p_j \, C_n & \text{if tier } j \text{ runs it and } j < J,\\
    E_J & \text{if } j = J \text{, the top tier},
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:cost-to-completion}
\end{equation}
where $E_j$ is the energy tier $j$ is expected to spend running the query. The last line
ends the recursion: the top tier $J$ has no tier above it to pass a query to, so it always
runs the query and returns its answer, and its cost is only its own energy. The cost of
every other tier depends on the costs of the tiers above it, so the costs are computed from
the top tier down, starting from $C_J = E_J$. We call a tier's energy per prompt token and per generated token its energy
rates, written $r^{\text{p}}_j$ and $r^{\text{g}}_j$, and the energy tier $j$ is expected
to spend on a query whose prompt has $P$ tokens is
\begin{equation}
  E_j = r^{\text{p}}_j \, P + r^{\text{g}}_j \, \bar{L}_j,
  \label{eq:expected-energy}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{L}_j$ is the average length of the tier's past answers, in tokens, which stands
in for the length of this answer because that length is only known after the tier runs.

The quantile test escalates about a fraction $\beta$ of a tier's answers by construction
(Section~\ref{sec:llm-background}), so WattLadder sets $p_j = \beta$. The policy does not
depend on this test, though, because Equation~\eqref{eq:cost-to-completion} needs only
the probability that a tier escalates, and so it works with any escalation test, such as
a fixed threshold or a separate scoring model~\cite{chen2024frugalgpt}. A test whose rate
is not fixed in advance would need $p_j$ to be learned from the answers instead, which is
why Equation~\eqref{eq:cost-to-completion} keeps $p_j$ apart from $\beta$.

The policy uses $C_j$ for two decisions in Algorithm~\ref{alg:decision}, both made when a
query arrives at a tier $i < J$. The first is whether to run the query at all (lines 2
to 5). Running it costs $E_i$ and, if its answer escalates, the expected energy of the
cheapest tier above, taken as $C_{k^*}$ even when the margin $\delta$ would keep the escalation
at tier $i+1$, so the tier forwards the query to $k^*$ without running it if
\begin{equation}
  C_{k^*} < (1-\delta)\left(E_i + p_i \, C_{k^*}\right),
  \label{eq:on-arrival}
\end{equation}
and runs it otherwise. The second is where the query goes if the tier runs it and its
answer fails the test (lines 10 to 12), which is tier $i+1$, or directly $k^*$ if
$C_{k^*} < (1-\delta)\,C_{i+1}$, with the energy rates the tier holds once it has finished the
query. Forwarding therefore spends nothing at tier $i$, while
escalating comes after the tier has spent $E_i$. The top tier has nowhere to send a query and
always returns its answer.

The margin $\delta$ keeps a tier from moving a query for a negligible saving, and it also
sets how far WattLadder departs from escalation by confidence alone. With $\delta = 0$, a
tier takes any route that is expected to cost less. With $\delta = 1$, neither
inequality can hold, since every energy is positive, so each tier runs every query that
reaches it and passes on only those whose answers fail the test, which is confidence-only
escalation exactly. The main results use $\delta = 0$, and Chapter~\ref{chap:results}
sweeps the values in between.

With $\delta = 0$ and $p_i = \beta$, Equation~\eqref{eq:on-arrival} becomes
$(1-\beta)\,C_{k^*} < E_i$, or $C_{k^*} < E_i / (1-\beta)$. Running the query costs
$E_i$, and it avoids the cost $C_{k^*}$ of the tiers above only when the answer passes
the test, which happens with probability $1-\beta$. A tier therefore runs a query only
if the cheapest tier above it costs at least $E_i/(1-\beta)$. This threshold rises with
$\beta$, because the more often a tier escalates, the more often the energy it spends
running a query is wasted.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
  \caption{The decision of tier $i < J$ on a query $q$}
  \label{alg:decision}
  \KwIn{the query $q$; the quantile $\beta$; the margin $\delta$; the window $W_i$ of
    tier $i$'s recent confidences; the average answer lengths of the tiers above; the
    energy rates of tier $i$ and of every tier above}
  $\mathit{next} \leftarrow i+1$\;
  compute $C_k$ for every $k > i$, from the top tier
  down\tcp*{Equation~\eqref{eq:cost-to-completion}}
  $k^* \leftarrow \arg\min_{k>i} C_k$\;
  \If(\tcp*[f]{Equation~\eqref{eq:on-arrival}}){$C_{k^*} < (1-\delta)\,(E_i + p_i\,C_{k^*})$}{
    forward $q$ to tier $k^*$ and stop\;
  }
  run $q$, giving the answer $a$ with confidence $s$\;
  $\theta \leftarrow$ the $\beta$-quantile of $W_i$, then add $s$ to $W_i$\;
  \If{$s \geq \theta$}{
    return $a$ to the user and stop\;
  }
  compute $C_k$ and $k^*$ again, with the energy rates it holds now\;
  \If{$C_{k^*} < (1-\delta)\,C_{i+1}$}{
    $\mathit{next} \leftarrow k^*$\;
  }
  pass $q$ to tier $\mathit{next}$\;
\end{algorithm}

busy, its 5~J cost less than what either lower tier would spend on an answer that still
escalates half of the time, so the ONU would forward the query and the user device sends
it straight to the OLT. When the OLT is idle, its 800~J are worth avoiding, so both lower
tiers run the query and it climbs one tier at a time, as under confidence alone. The same
query therefore takes the shortest route when the OLT is busy and the longest when it is
idle, which is why the tiers need a current estimate of the OLT's cost.

To compute $C_k$, a tier needs three pieces of information from the other tiers: their average answer
lengths, the energy rates of the user device and the ONU, and the current energy rate of
the OLT. The quantile test needs nothing from them, since each device keeps its own window
of its last 1,000 confidences, within the 300 to 1,000 that \citeonline{wu2025recserve}
find sufficient, started from the confidences of its model on the benchmark so that the
threshold works from the first query.

For the answer lengths, each tier ships with the mean answer length of its model, measured
on the benchmark before deployment, and then moves this average 5\% of the way toward the
length $L$ of each new answer, $\bar{L}_j \leftarrow 0.95\,\bar{L}_j + 0.05\,L$, so that each
older answer counts 5\% less than the one after it and the last 20 answers carry about two
thirds of the weight. Since an answer travels back to the user through every tier below the
one that produced it, it can carry these averages at no extra cost, and each tier below
replaces the lengths it holds with the ones it hears. The OLT's average covers every
household it serves, while those of the user device and the ONU cover only their own
household.

The energy rates of the user device and the ONU do not change, and answers carry them in
the same way, so the rates a tier hears on past answers stay valid. The OLT's rate, however,
depends on how many other queries the OLT is serving at the moment
(Section~\ref{sec:olt-energy}), and a household hears it on its own answers only a few
times a day. We compare two strategies for giving the tiers a current estimate of it:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Timetable.} Every device receives in advance a table with one OLT rate for
  each hour of the day, one for weekdays and one for weekends, built over a month of
  calibration. Building it is not straightforward, because the rate the OLT reports
  depends on the table itself. If the table says the OLT is expensive at some hour, the
  tiers below answer the queries themselves, the OLT receives few of them and stays idle,
  and an idle OLT really is expensive, while a table that says the OLT is cheap sends it
  enough queries to keep it busy and cheap. We therefore build the table in rounds. The
  first table sets every hour to $r_{\text{busy}}$ (Section~\ref{sec:accounting}), the
  lowest rate the OLT can report, and each round replays the calibration month with the
  policy using the current table, records the rate the OLT reports in each hour, and takes
  these rates as the next table. We stop when the table changes by less than 1\% on
  average from one round to the next, or after 12 rounds. Starting from the lowest rate
  keeps the table from settling on rates that are high only because they keep the OLT
  idle.
  \item \textbf{Broadcast.} Every 10 seconds, the OLT sends downstream the energy one
  more query would add to its consumption, averaged over its last 5 minutes
  (Section~\ref{sec:accounting}). The 5 minutes smooth out the batches that form and
  dissolve within seconds, and sending every 10 seconds keeps the figure current at the
  cost of one small message per PON port, a negligible load on its downstream. A single message on each of its PON ports reaches every
  ONU on that port, and each ONU passes it on to the devices in its home. Before the
  OLT's first message, a tier has no figure to use, so it runs every query that reaches it
  and escalates by confidence alone.
\end{itemize}

\section{Energy Model}
\label{sec:energy-model}

The energy of every tier is meant to be counted at the same boundary: all the energy the
system draws to answer a query, as in Google's full-stack accounting of a
prompt~\cite{elsworth2025measuring}. The phone's source covers only its chip, so the user
device is counted below this boundary (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). The ONU's source, by
contrast, measures it directly at the plug of the whole board, and the OLT's GPU card is
brought to it with Google's shares of a prompt's energy and the industry's average PUE,
which add an estimate of the rest of its server, the spare machines kept ready for peaks
and the building that houses them. The energy of carrying queries and answers over the PON
and the home network is left out, as are relaying the OLT's broadcast inside the home and
manufacturing the hardware.

The energy of a query also depends on how it is charged, and we consider two ways of
charging it (Section~\ref{sec:measurements}): average accounting, which counts everything a
tier draws while it answers, and marginal accounting, which counts only what it draws above idle, so
nothing is charged for keeping the hardware on. We assume that the hardware stays on wherever a
query goes, so the power it draws at rest is spent either way and a routing decision changes
only the energy above idle. The tiers therefore decide on energy above idle under both
accountings. The study charges under marginal accounting, and its main runs are repeated under
average accounting as a sensitivity analysis, with the queries routed the same way, so that only
the energy counted changes. Table~\ref{tab:energy} lists
what each source measures and how close it comes to the boundary, together with the energy
each tier spends on a query of its mean prompt and answer lengths on GSM8K under both
accountings. The OLT's energy depends on how many queries it batches, so the table gives its
range, which Sections~\ref{sec:olt-energy} and~\ref{sec:accounting} explain.

0.016~J per prompt token and 0.074~J per generated token, or

126 prompt and 185 generated tokens (Table~\ref{tab:user-onu}). This figure is charged in full under both accountings, since
the source gives no idle power to subtract, and it covers the chip but not the memory, two
limitations discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

The energy of the ONU comes from the Raspberry Pi 5 with a Hailo-10H accelerator that
\citeonline{renney2026cloud} measured with a plug-in power meter while it ran

separately. \citeonline{tummalapalli2026edge} timed the same board reading a prompt of
258 tokens in 1.287~s, about 200 tokens per second, and assuming the board draws the same
power while reading as while generating, a prompt token costs $5.58/200 = 0.028$~J, or
$2.08/200 = 0.010$~J above idle. With the mean prompt of 122 tokens, a query then costs
the ONU $243 + 122 \times 0.028 = 246$~J, or $91 + 122 \times 0.010 = 92$~J above idle.

\subsection{Measuring the OLT}
\label{sec:olt-energy}

We measured the energy of the OLT ourselves, running its model, Qwen2.5-7B in 8-bit, on
one NVIDIA L4 GPU rented in the cloud, served with vLLM 0.21.0~\cite{kwon2023vllm} under
the NVIDIA driver 610.57.04 and with prefix caching disabled, so that every run reads its
prompts again instead of reusing them. The GPU keeps a running total of the energy it has used, which NVML reports,
and we read this total before and after each run and took the difference. Software
readings like this are accurate on GPUs~\cite{chung2025mlenergy}, and the running total
avoids the power sampling that, on GPUs such as the A100 and H100, was found to cover only
a quarter of the runtime~\cite{yang2023part}.

We gave the GPU static batches of 1 to 64 queries, in which every query starts and ends
together, drawn from 64 GSM8K problems whose prompts average 92 tokens, and each query
was made to generate exactly 300 tokens, near the 251 tokens of the OLT's average answer on
GSM8K. To separate the two
phases of inference (Section~\ref{sec:llm-background}), each batch was also run
generating a single token, which measures the prefill alone, and the decode is the
difference between the full run and this one. Every measurement was repeated five times after one
warm-up run, and we kept the median. We also measured the GPU at rest, at 32.1~W with the
model loaded, so that the energy spent above this idle power could be computed (the last
two columns of Table~\ref{tab:olt-sweep}).

Batching amortizes the GPU's energy across its queries, since the number of tokens the L4
generates per second grows from 29.1 for a query alone to 1,394.2 in a batch of 64
(Table~\ref{tab:olt-sweep}) while its power stays at its limit of 72~W. The speed grows 48
times for a batch 64 times larger because each decode step is limited by memory
bandwidth (Section~\ref{sec:llm-background}), and a batch reads the model's weights once for
all its queries. It falls short of 64 times in part because the GPU already reaches its
power limit with a single query, so as the batch grows it lowers its clock instead of
drawing more. With the two phases separated, the decode costs 2.464~J per generated token
alone and 0.047~J in a batch of 64, about 50 times less. The
prefill, which is limited by computation rather than memory
(Section~\ref{sec:llm-background}), gains far less, falling from 0.033~J per prompt token
to about 0.015~J by a batch of 16 and staying there.

\subsection{Cost per Query at the OLT}
\label{sec:accounting}

The GPU is only part of what the OLT's server consumes, so we multiply its energy by
2.44 to cover the rest. In Google's measurements, the accelerators account for 58\% of a
prompt's energy, the server's CPU and memory for 24\%, the spare machines kept ready for
peaks for 10\% and the building for 8\%~\cite{elsworth2025measuring}. Counting the first
three, the computing equipment spends $(58+24+10)/58 = 1.586$ times the energy of its
accelerators, and
using the industry's average PUE of 1.54~\cite{uptime2025survey} in place of Google's
building overhead gives $1.586 \times 1.54 = 2.44$. Chapter~\ref{chap:results} repeats the
main runs with three other factors, to show how much the results depend on this one: 1.0 for
the GPU card alone, $100/58 = 1.72$ for Google's own accounting of a prompt, and
$(58+58+10)/58 \times 1.54 = 3.35$ for a host that draws as much as its card.

Under both accountings, a query at the OLT is charged its share of its batch's energy, read
from Table~\ref{tab:olt-sweep} at the batch's size and multiplied by 2.44.
A query of the OLT's mean lengths on GSM8K, 122 prompt and 251 generated tokens, costs
$(0.033 \times 122 + 2.464 \times 251) \times 2.44 \approx 1{,}520$~J alone under average
accounting and $(0.018 \times 122 + 1.365 \times 251) \times 2.44 \approx 840$~J alone under
marginal accounting. In a batch of 64, the same query costs
$(0.014 \times 122 + 0.047 \times 251) \times 2.44 \approx 33$~J under average accounting and
$(0.008 \times 122 + 0.026 \times 251) \times 2.44 \approx 18$~J under marginal accounting.

These figures are what a query is charged once it has run, but a tier decides where to send a
query before the OLT has seen it. The OLT therefore has to tell the tiers below what a query
would cost, and it could report two different things. The first is
the query's share, the batch's energy divided among its queries, which under marginal
accounting is about 18~J in a batch of 64. The second is what the query adds, the difference
between the OLT's energy with the query and without it, which in the same batch is only about
5~J. A batch of 64 costs about 1,180~J above idle, or about 18~J per query, against the 840~J
of one query alone, so each of the other 63 adds $(1{,}180 - 840)/63 \approx 5$~J. A query that
finds the OLT idle, in contrast, adds the full 840~J, since without it the GPU would be at rest.

The OLT reports what a query adds above idle (Section~\ref{sec:energy-model}), since sending a
query to the OLT changes its energy by that amount, while the query's share also carries part
of what its batch would spend without it. A tier cannot know whether its query will find the OLT idle or busy, so the OLT weights the two cases by the fraction $b$ of
the last 5 minutes it spent busy, with at least one query generating, and reports the energy rate
\begin{equation}
  r = b \, r_{\text{busy}} + (1-b) \, r_{\text{idle}},
  \label{eq:olt-report}
\end{equation}
for prompt and generated tokens separately, where $r_{\text{idle}}$ is the rate of a query that
finds the OLT idle and $r_{\text{busy}}$ is the rate of a query that joins a running batch.
Both rates are fixed, so only $b$ changes over time. For a query of the mean lengths, they come
to about 840~J and 5~J, and an OLT that was busy half of the time reports
$0.5 \times 5 + 0.5 \times 840 \approx 420$~J.

The idle rate $r_{\text{idle}}$ is read from Table~\ref{tab:olt-sweep} at a batch of one and
multiplied by 2.44, which gives 0.044~J per prompt token and 3.33~J per generated token.
The busy rate $r_{\text{busy}}$ follows the idea of the calculation above, which used only the two ends of the table,
but draws on every measured size. Separately for prompt and generated tokens, we take the energy
above idle of a whole batch at each size, divide it by the tokens of one query, multiply it by
2.44 and fit a least-squares line of the result against the batch size, over batches 1 to 64
(Figure~\ref{fig:batch-energy}). The slopes are what each extra query adds, 0.0185~J per prompt
token and 0.0122~J per generated token, so that the rate can price queries of any length, and
a query of the mean lengths adds $0.0185 \times 122 + 0.0122 \times 251 \approx 5$~J. We fit a single slope because the energy added between adjacent measured sizes is noisy
at small batches and, for such a query, stays between about 4 and 6~J above four queries.

The OLT sends its report on the broadcast and also stamps it on the
answers it returns. The timetable holds the same report, computed with Equation~\eqref{eq:olt-report} over all the
hours of the calibration month that share a day type and an hour of the day, such as its 20
weekday hours from 14:00 to 15:00, instead of over the last 5 minutes, so the two strategies differ only in whether the report is live or learned in advance. Under the broadcast strategy
the tiers take the OLT's cost only from the broadcast, and Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} tests
relying on the answers alone, to show what the broadcast adds.

\section{Simulation}
\label{sec:simulation}

The simulator we built for the case study follows one OLT and the households it serves
through time, from the moment
each household sends a message until some tier returns its answer. It runs no model,
since every answer is the one recorded for its question (Section~\ref{sec:overview}), and
it charges each tier the energy of Section~\ref{sec:energy-model}, so what it reproduces
is when the queries arrive, where each policy sends them and how busy the OLT is when
they get there.

The simulator has five modules, the solid boxes of Figure~\ref{fig:simulator}, while the dashed
boxes are its data. The traffic generator builds the
messages the households send, from the data and the drift of
Sections~\ref{sec:traffic} and~\ref{sec:drift}. The event loop is its core, and since the simulator
is event-driven, the clock jumps from one event to the next, in time
order: a message being sent, a query reaching a tier, or a tier finishing a query, the OLT
included. A query reaches a tier only after the seconds the tiers below spent on it, and each
tier then asks the routing policy, which applies Algorithm~\ref{alg:decision} with what the tier
knows at that moment. A query that reaches the OLT joins the batch held by the OLT server,
which charges the query's energy (Section~\ref{sec:serving}) and computes the OLT's report, also
sending it every 10 seconds under the broadcast strategy. When an answer reaches the user, the event loop records the query's
energy, accuracy, latency and bytes, and queues the next message of its conversation, if there
is one, a pause later. Throughout, the energy model gives the event loop and the OLT server the
energy rates and speeds they need (Section~\ref{sec:energy-model}).

\subsection{Generating the Traffic}
\label{sec:traffic}

How people chat comes from the WildChat-4.8M release of WildChat, the logs of a free ChatGPT
service used around the world~\cite{zhao2024wildchat}. WildChat records its times on the server's
clock, and its users are spread over the world, so each user's times are first moved to their
own time zone, and every simulated household then shares that one local day, as the households of
one OLT share a time zone. Users who sent a single message are left out, because WildChat's sudden
traffic spikes are made mostly of them, which suggests automated traffic rather than people, and
so are the messages whose time zone cannot be told. The simulator then builds the traffic of a run
in the following order:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Households.} Each run connects 1,000, 2,000, 5,000, 10,000 or 20,000 households to
  the OLT, one ONU each, which needs at least 8 PON ports for the smallest and 157 for the largest,
  since G-PON allows at most 128 ONUs per port. Each household has a number of people who use an LLM
  every week, drawn from a Poisson distribution with a mean of one. We assume this mean because we
  found no measured figure for Brazilian households, and since the load depends on the number of
  users, the sweep of households also shows what a different mean would do.
  \item \textbf{User activity.} Some people chat far more than others, and in WildChat we found that
  the busiest 10\% of the users active in two or more different weeks send 59\% of the messages.
  Each simulated user is therefore given a random level of activity from a log-normal distribution
  with a mean of one and a log standard deviation of 1.5, so that the busiest 10\% send the same
  share. A typical user then sends about a third of the average, while the busiest 1\% send more
  than ten times the average. A household's share
  of the traffic is the sum of its users' levels divided by the sum over all households, so the
  levels decide only who sends the messages and not how many there are.
  \item \textbf{Conversations per day.} A user sends 3.6 messages a day on average, the mean of
  ChatGPT's users in July 2025, when more than 700 million users a week together sent more than 2.5
  billion messages a day~\cite{chatterji2025how}. People send them in conversations, and a
  conversation, one chat as WildChat records it, holds 2.4 messages on average, so 10,000 users
  open about $10{,}000 \times 3.6 / 2.4 = 15{,}000$ conversations a day.
  \item \textbf{Conversations per hour.} These conversations follow WildChat's average day
  (Figure~\ref{fig:traffic-profiles}), in which
  the busiest hour of a weekday, in the afternoon, sees about five times as many conversations start
  as the quietest, before dawn, and a weekend day has about five sixths of the conversations of a
  weekday. The expected number of conversations in each hour is multiplied by the drift of
  Section~\ref{sec:drift}, and how many of them start in that hour is then drawn from a Poisson
  distribution with this mean, each at a random moment within the hour.
  \item \textbf{Length and pauses.} Each conversation draws its number of messages at random from
  those of WildChat's conversations, of which 63\% hold a single message, even with the users who
  sent only one left out, and about one in eight holds five or more. Each follow-up then draws its own pause from the time between two answers of the same
  WildChat conversation, about two minutes at the median, since WildChat records only when an
  answer arrived.
  \item \textbf{Sender and questions.} Each conversation goes to one household, drawn in
  proportion to the households' shares, and each of its messages asks a question drawn at random from
  GSM8K.
  \item \textbf{Follow-ups and context.} During the run, a follow-up is sent a pause after the previous answer
  arrives, with a prompt that carries the earlier turns of its conversation, so prompts grow longer
  as a conversation goes on.
\end{enumerate}

Drawing the conversation starts from a Poisson process with an hourly rate follows the way
sessions started by users are usually modeled~\cite{paxson1995wide}. BurstGPT's
conversation log, from one regional GPT service that an organization such as a company or a
campus built on Azure OpenAI~\cite{wang2025burstgpt}, supports it, since we found that in its
busy hours the time between conversation starts varies as much as in a Poisson process, with a
coefficient of variation of 0.98 against 1, while its messages arrive in clusters mostly because
each conversation sends several within a few minutes.

WildChat's Brazilian users follow almost the same daily shape as its users worldwide
(Figure~\ref{fig:traffic-profiles}), so the worldwide traffic stands in for Brazilian
households. BurstGPT's day instead follows office hours, with peaks in the morning and the
afternoon and little traffic in the evening, which is why it gives the study only the size and
the duration of the drift (Section~\ref{sec:drift}).

\subsection{Adding Drift to the Traffic}
\label{sec:drift}

If every day followed the average day, a timetable would predict the OLT's load almost
perfectly, since an hour's load would differ from it only by the randomness of the arrivals.
Real load drifts, with hours that run busier or quieter than usual, so the study has
three scenarios, of which Figure~\ref{fig:traffic-week} shows one test week. With no
drift every day follows the average day, while with mild and strong drift the load of
all households is multiplied by a common busyness factor that changes slowly over time.
The factor's mean is one, so the month carries the same number of messages on average, but an hour
can run, for example, a quarter busier than the timetable says and stay so for some
hours before returning to it.

Such drift is well documented in call centers, whose arrivals have been studied most closely,
since the count of a period there varies more than a Poisson process with a fixed timetable
allows and busy periods persist. The usual model there multiplies the timetable by a random
busyness factor whose mean is one and draws the arrivals from a Poisson process at that
rate~\cite{ibrahim2012modeling}. The factor can stay the same for the whole day or change from
one period to the next while depending on the periods before it, and ours is of the
second kind. Our measurement below finds the same excess in
BurstGPT, whose hourly counts of conversation starts vary around their timetable more than
the randomness of the arrivals alone explains, and whose authors find the burstiness of its requests changing from
one twenty-minute window to the next during working hours~\cite{wang2025burstgpt}.

In our simulation the factor $F_k$
changes every hour $k$ and is shared by every household of the OLT, which makes the
conversation starts a log-Gaussian Cox process~\cite{moller1998log}, and it is built from a
number $z_k$ that keeps part of the previous hour's departure and receives a random kick
each hour,
as
\begin{equation}
  z_k = \phi\, z_{k-1} + \varepsilon_k, \qquad F_k = e^{\,z_k - \sigma^2/2},
  \label{eq:drift}
\end{equation}
where $\phi$ is the share of the departure that the next hour keeps and $\varepsilon_k$ is the
random kick, drawn from a normal distribution with a mean of zero and a standard deviation of
$\sigma\sqrt{1-\phi^2}$. The kicks then keep the standard deviation of $z_k$ at $\sigma$, which
sets how large a typical departure is, while $\tau$, in hours, sets how long it lasts.
Setting $\phi = e^{-1/\tau}$ makes $\phi^{\tau} = e^{-1}$, so a departure that receives no
new kicks shrinks to about 37\% of its size after $\tau$ hours.
The exponential turns $z_k$ into a multiplier, so that equal steps up and down in $z_k$
make the load equally many times higher or lower, and subtracting $\sigma^2/2$ keeps the
mean of $F_k$ at one. With the values of the mild drift given below, for example,
$\phi = 0.68$, so an hour with $z_k = 0.30$, whose load is 31\% above the timetable, is
followed by hours with $z_k = 0.20$ and $0.14$, 18\% and then 11\% above it, if no new kick
arrives.

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \caption{Messages per hour over one test week of 10,000 households}
  \label{fig:traffic-week}
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/traffic_week.pdf}
  \legend{Source: The author}
\end{figure}

We measured $\sigma$ and $\tau$ on BurstGPT, whose more than 3,000 users reached the
models through one regional GPT service, built on Azure OpenAI by a company or
campus~\cite{wang2025burstgpt}. These users share one service, which brings them
closer to the users of a single OLT than WildChat's, who are spread over the world and do
not share one access network. They reach the models from work or study rather than from
home, though, so their load may drift differently from that of a household. We used the
conversation log of one file of BurstGPT's second release, which spans 110 consecutive
days, and took each of its sessions as one conversation, as its authors
count them and as WildChat's records are. We then compared the number of conversations $N$
that start in each hour with its timetable $\lambda$, the mean count of that hour over the
days of the same type, weekday or weekend. The drift is the factor $F_k$ of
Equation~\eqref{eq:drift} that would produce these counts. The variance of a Poisson count
with a random rate is the variance of its rate plus its mean~\cite{ibrahim2012modeling}, and since
$F_k$ is log-normal with a mean of one, its variance is $e^{\sigma^2} - 1$, which gives
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{E}\!\left[\left(\frac{N}{\lambda} - 1\right)^{2}\right] = \left(e^{\sigma^2} - 1\right) + \frac{1}{\lambda},
  \label{eq:drift-size}
\end{equation}
so $\sigma$ follows from the mean squared departure of the hours once $1/\lambda$, the part
that the randomness of counting alone explains, is subtracted. Only the hours with at least
20 conversations expected are used, because in quieter hours the departure is mostly
counting noise. The duration
$\tau$ comes from how closely the departures of consecutive hours follow each other, and
the code that computes both is part of the public repository.

BurstGPT's traffic also grew over those 110 days, so its late weeks run above the timetable
in almost every hour and its early weeks below it, and measured as it is, this slow growth
would count as drift. For the mild drift, each day's timetable is therefore first scaled by
how busy its week was, the mean ratio between the counts and the timetable over the seven
days around it. In a week whose days run 30\% above the timetable, for example, an hour that
usually has 50 conversations is expected to have 65, so a count of 70 is a departure of 8\%
rather than 40\%, and only the surprises within each week count as drift. This gives
$\sigma = 0.253$ and $\tau = 2.6$ hours, so a typical hour, with $z_k$
one $\sigma$ above or below zero, runs about 25\% busier or quieter than its timetable, since
$e^{0.253 - 0.032} = 1.25$ and $e^{-0.253 - 0.032} = 0.75$, and such a departure lasts about
two and a half hours. Without this weekly scaling, $\sigma = 0.477$ and $\tau = 9.4$ hours, so a
typical hour runs about 44\% busier or 45\% quieter for about nine hours, since
$e^{0.477 - 0.114} = 1.44$ and $e^{-0.477 - 0.114} = 0.55$, which gives the strong drift. Most of this larger departure is the growth, which the
simulation turns into random swings of about nine hours, so the strong drift is best read as
a demanding case. The calibration month
drifts as well, with the same $\sigma$ and $\tau$ as the test month but its own random
departures, so the timetable learns the average of each hour, drift included, but not when the
next departure will come.

\subsection{Serving the Queries}
\label{sec:serving}

The user device and the ONU answer at the speeds their sources measured
(Section~\ref{sec:user-onu-energy}). The phone reads the prompt at 288 tokens per second and
generates 72 tokens per second, so an answer of its mean length takes it about 2.6 seconds,
while the ONU reads the prompt at about 200 tokens per second and generates 6.3 tokens per
second, so an answer of its mean length takes it about 44 seconds, and a longer prompt takes
either of them longer. Each household's
ONU answers one query at a time, in the order they reach it,
so a second query from the same household waits for the first. The OLT serves with continuous
batching (Section~\ref{sec:olt-energy}), with 64 slots, the largest batch measured, and a
query that finds every slot busy waits for one. A query reaches the OLT only after the
seconds the tiers below spent on it, so the batch it meets is the one actually running
at that moment, and its prefill is charged at the size of that batch. Two short delays are
left out of the timing: the OLT's prefill, which is short next to its decode, and the few
milliseconds spent crossing the network.


\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

A policy that escalates more queries reaches larger models and answers more of them
correctly, so comparing policies at the same $\beta$ would mix how much energy each spends
with how accurate it is. We therefore compare them at the same accuracy, with a target of
80\% in the main results. Every policy runs at
nine values of $\beta$, from 0.1 to 0.9, each giving one pair of accuracy and energy per
query, and we drop any pair that another value of $\beta$ beats on both, with higher
accuracy for less energy. The energy a policy needs to reach a target accuracy is then
interpolated linearly between the two remaining pairs whose accuracies lie on either side of
the target. A policy whose most accurate pair is below the target is reported as not
reaching it, and one whose least accurate pair is already above the target is read at that
pair. WattLadder weighs energy alone and leaves judging the
answers to the quantile test, so it changes accuracy only through which models answer, and
reading every policy at the same accuracy accounts for that change.

WattLadder is evaluated with both strategies of Section~\ref{sec:policy} for estimating the
OLT's cost, the timetable and the broadcast, against three baselines. The first is
escalation by confidence alone, the rule of \citeonline{wu2025recserve}, in which each tier
answers or passes a query up by the quantile test only. The second is the same rule without
the ONU, so that a query goes from the phone straight to the OLT, which shows how much of a
saving comes from skipping the ONU rather than from weighing energy. The third is each tier
answering every query alone, which gives the energy and accuracy of the phone, the ONU and
the OLT on their own. A further policy, the piggyback, removes the broadcast, so each
household learns the OLT's cost only from the OLT's answers to its own queries and blends
each figure it hears into its estimate with a weight of 0.3, and until it hears the first
one, it escalates by confidence alone. It shows how much the broadcast
adds over what a household learns by itself.

Each run reports four measures over its test month, each the mean over the three seeds:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Energy per query}, the month's total energy divided by its number of queries,
  under the marginal accounting of Section~\ref{sec:accounting}.
  \item \textbf{Accuracy}, the share of questions answered correctly.
  \item \textbf{Latency}, the time from a message being sent to its answer arriving.
  \item \textbf{Communication burden}, the measure of \citeonline{wu2025recserve}, the bytes
  of a query's question and answer counted at both the sending and the receiving node of
  every hop the query climbs.
\end{itemize}

The latency and the communication burden are read at the same point as the energy, so each
policy is described at the setting it would use to reach the target accuracy.

The policies run under the three scenarios of Section~\ref{sec:drift}, with no, mild and
strong drift, at 1,000, 2,000, 5,000, 10,000 and 20,000 households. Five more runs, without
drift, each change one assumption. Three price the OLT differently from the $\times 2.44$ of
Section~\ref{sec:accounting}: at $\times 1.0$ for the GPU card alone, $\times 1.72$ for
Google's own accounting and $\times 3.35$ for a host that draws as much as its card. The
other two change one thing each: one makes the ONU twice as efficient, and the other charges
the same routing under average accounting, which changes the totals but no decision. Finally, at 10,000 households and without drift, a sweep of the margin $\delta$ through 0, 0.05,
0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7 and 1 moves WattLadder with the broadcast step by step from weighing
energy alone to escalation by confidence alone, which $\delta = 1$ reproduces exactly.

%==============================================================================
\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}
%==============================================================================

This chapter reports the study of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}, with every number read at an
accuracy of 80\% and averaged over the three seeds unless stated otherwise.
Section~\ref{sec:results-energy} compares the energy each policy consumes to reach that
accuracy, and Section~\ref{sec:results-strategies} the two ways of estimating the OLT's cost.
Section~\ref{sec:results-traffic} then turns to the latency and the traffic that come with the
saving, Section~\ref{sec:results-delta} to the margin $\delta$ and
Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} to the sensitivity runs, and Section~\ref{sec:limitations}
gathers the limitations of the study.

\section{Energy at the Same Accuracy}
\label{sec:results-energy}

The energy WattLadder saves depends on how busy the OLT can be, and it grows with the number
of households (Figure~\ref{fig:saving-households}). At 1,000 and 2,000 households it saves
less than 1\%, since the OLT almost never holds two queries at once and every answer it gives pays
the cost of a lone query, so moving work to it gains nothing. From 5,000 households onward,
the OLT starts to batch, and WattLadder spends 10.3\% less energy than escalation by confidence alone
with the timetable and 11.1\% less with the broadcast. The saving reaches 43.1\% and 30.4\% at
10,000 households and 51.8\% and 46.5\% at 20,000, and the three seeds differ by at most 1.2
percentage points. At 20,000 households the timetable reaches 85\% accuracy already at
$\beta = 0.1$, its lowest value, and about 81 to 84\% under drift, so its figures there are read
at that point (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}), and its saving comes with more accuracy than
confidence alone delivers.

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \caption{Energy saved over confidence alone, by number of households}
  \label{fig:saving-households}
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/saving_over_recserve.pdf}
  \legend{Source: The author}
\end{figure}

At 10,000 households with no drift, escalation by confidence alone needs 322~J per query to
reach 80\% accuracy, while WattLadder needs 183~J with the timetable and 224~J with the broadcast
(Figure~\ref{fig:equal-accuracy}). Part of this saving comes from skipping the ONU, but not all
of it, since escalating by confidence alone straight from the phone to the OLT still needs
260~J, and WattLadder spends 30\% less than that with the timetable and 14\% less with the
broadcast. The rest comes from weighing the OLT's cost, which sends queries to the OLT when it
batches and keeps them on the phone when it does not. Sending every query to the
OLT costs 257~J, so WattLadder also spends 29\% and 13\% less than the OLT alone, by keeping on
the phone the queries the phone answers with confidence. The OLT alone is more accurate,
though, at 91.7\%, and near that accuracy WattLadder spends about as much as it does.

Neither of these fixed rules is the best choice at every size. At 1,000 households, sending
every query to the OLT costs 748~J, 39\% more than confidence alone, because the OLT rarely
batches, while at 20,000 households it costs 147~J, 38\% less. WattLadder matches the better of
the two when the OLT is idle and spends less than both when the OLT batches.

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \caption{Energy per query against accuracy at 10,000 households}
  \label{fig:equal-accuracy}
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/equal_accuracy.pdf}
  \legend{Source: The author}
\end{figure}

Most of the saving comes from the tiers below the OLT (Figure~\ref{fig:energy-split}). Under
confidence alone, a query that ends at the OLT has already been answered by the phone and the
ONU, and that work is wasted, so the ONU alone accounts for 74~J of the 322~J. With the
broadcast and $\delta = 0$, the phone and the ONU spend 17~J and 20~J instead of 34~J and 74~J,
and the OLT spends less as well, 188~J instead of 214~J, even though it answers more queries,
because they batch.

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \caption{Energy per query spent at each tier at 10,000 households}
  \label{fig:energy-split}
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/energy_split.pdf}
  \legend{Source: The author}
\end{figure}

In absolute terms, the saving is modest for a single OLT. A test month of 28 days carries about
1.0 million queries at 10,000 households, so the timetable saves about 39~kWh and the broadcast
about 27~kWh, and at 20,000 households, with about 2.0 million queries, it saves about 69~kWh
and 62~kWh. The saving grows with the number of queries, since more queries both add up and
batch more, and an operator runs many OLTs.

\section{The Timetable and the Broadcast}
\label{sec:results-strategies}

Of the two ways of estimating the OLT's cost, the timetable spends less energy in most cases
(Figure~\ref{fig:broadcast-timetable}). With no drift, the broadcast spends 22.3\% more than
the timetable at 10,000 households and 11.1\% more at 20,000, and up to 5,000 households the
two coincide. The broadcast spends less only under drift, by 3.4\% with mild drift at 5,000
households, and with strong drift by 3.3\% at 2,000, 9.1\% at 5,000 and 11.9\% at 10,000.

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \caption{Energy the broadcast saves over the timetable, by households and drift}
  \label{fig:broadcast-timetable}
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/broadcast_over_timetable.pdf}
  \legend{Source: The author}
\end{figure}

The reason lies in how the OLT's cost depends on the load the policy itself sends it
(Figure~\ref{fig:beta-knob}). At a low $\beta$ few queries escalate, so the OLT is mostly idle,
and the broadcast reports, correctly, that one more query would cost it a lot. No tier then
forwards or skips a query to it, so it receives only what escalates by confidence and stays
idle and expensive, and from $\beta = 0.1$ to 0.3 WattLadder with the
broadcast takes exactly the routes of confidence alone, with the same energy and accuracy. At the
same low $\beta$, the timetable behaves the other way, since it starts every hour at the cost
of a busy OLT and is relearned until it agrees with the load it produces
(Section~\ref{sec:policy}). Households therefore send work to the OLT from
the start, which keeps it busy enough to batch about four queries at a time, so its cost
really is low. The OLT's
cost therefore has two states that are each consistent with the routing they cause, an idle
and expensive one and a busy and cheap one. A live report stays in the first when little
load escalates, while a timetable that starts from the second reaches it. From $\beta = 0.5$
on, enough queries escalate for the broadcast to see a busy OLT too, and the two strategies
spend within 1\% of each other. To reach 80\% accuracy, the timetable therefore needs only
$\beta \approx 0.2$, since it already sends most queries to the OLT, while the broadcast needs
$\beta \approx 0.5$, where it has left the idle state but still tries more queries on the phone
and the ONU first.

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \caption{Accuracy (a) and energy per query (b) against $\beta$}
  \label{fig:beta-knob}
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/beta_knob.pdf}
  \legend{Source: The author}
\end{figure}

Drift works the other way (Figure~\ref{fig:drift}). At 10,000 households, the timetable needs
183~J with no drift, 197~J with mild drift and 224~J with strong drift, since its expectation
is wrong more often as the load departs from its usual pattern, while the broadcast needs
224~J, 217~J and 197~J, since it sees each departure as it happens. Whether the broadcast beats
the timetable therefore depends on two opposite effects: what it gains by following the drift,
and what it loses by starting from an idle OLT. Both depend on the number of households, since
following the drift only helps when a departure moves the OLT between batching and idling,
which changes where a query should go. At 5,000 households the OLT sits just below the load at
which batching starts to pay, batching 1.8 queries on average under either strategy, against
4.2 with the timetable and 3.3 with the broadcast at 10,000, so a busy start gains the timetable
little and an idle start costs the broadcast little, and with no drift the two coincide. Under drift, busy
spells push the OLT into batching for a while, and since only the broadcast notices them, its
advantage grows with the drift. At 10,000 households
starting from an idle OLT costs the broadcast more, and mild drift does not make up for it,
since its dips of about 25\% still leave the OLT batching. Only strong drift, with dips of about
45\% that last some hours, leaves the OLT idle while the timetable keeps sending it work. At 20,000
households, the OLT keeps batching even in those
dips, so the timetable's expectation holds and the broadcast only pays for its idle start. A
sudden and much deeper drop, such as a failed PON port or a power cut, is the extreme of such
a dip, where the broadcast should gain most, although our scenarios do not include one.

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \caption{Energy per query with no, mild and strong drift}
  \label{fig:drift}
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/drift.pdf}
  \legend{Source: The author}
\end{figure}

The piggyback shows what the broadcast adds over what a household learns by itself. A
household that learns the OLT's cost only from the answers to its own queries hears it just a
few times a day, too rarely to follow it. The broadcast therefore spends 10.1\% to 13.6\% less
than the piggyback at 10,000 households and 11.5\% to 15.7\% less at 20,000, depending on the
drift.

The timetable is learned here on the same OLT, number of households and $\beta$ it is tested
on, which is the best case for it. An OLT whose number of clients changes, or a timetable
shared across OLTs, would need the timetable relearned, while the broadcast needs no training and
follows such changes as they happen.

\section{Latency and Communication Burden}
\label{sec:results-traffic}

The energy WattLadder saves costs little traffic (Figure~\ref{fig:bandwidth}). At 10,000
households, the communication burden at 80\% accuracy is 10.8~MB per 1,000 queries under
confidence alone and 11.8~MB under WattLadder with either strategy, 9\% more, while sending
every query to the OLT gives 17.4~MB, 60\% more. At 20,000 households the two strategies part,
since the timetable, read at the 85\% accuracy it reaches with $\beta = 0.1$, sends most queries
to the OLT and has a burden of 13.7~MB, 13\% more than the broadcast's 12.1~MB at 80\%, for 10\%
less energy.

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \caption{Communication burden against accuracy (a), and change in energy and burden against confidence alone (b)}
  \label{fig:bandwidth}
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/bandwidth.pdf}
  \legend{Source: The author}
\end{figure}

The saving does not cost waiting time either. Under confidence alone a message waits 47.6~s on
average for its answer at 10,000 households, mostly because many queries are answered by the
ONU or pass through it, at about 44~s per answer. WattLadder skips the ONU more often, so a
message waits 12.7~s with the timetable and 18.5~s with the broadcast, against 8.8~s when the
OLT answers every query. The ONU's slowness remains a limitation for the queries it does
answer (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{The Margin \texorpdfstring{$\delta$}{delta}}
\label{sec:results-delta}

The margin $\delta$ moves WattLadder from weighing energy alone to escalation by confidence
alone, trading energy against traffic (Figure~\ref{fig:delta}). At 10,000 households with the
broadcast, $\delta = 0$ saves 30.4\% of the energy for 9\% more traffic and a wait 61\% shorter,
$\delta = 0.3$ saves 21.7\% for 4\% more traffic, with more of its energy spent at the phone
and the ONU (Figure~\ref{fig:energy-split}), and $\delta = 0.5$ still saves 12.3\% while
carrying 4\% less traffic than confidence alone, with $\delta = 1$ reproducing confidence alone
exactly. With $\delta = 0.5$ the traffic is lower because the OLT's larger model answers most
of the queries WattLadder escalates, so it reaches 80\% with a lower $\beta$ and more queries
stay at the phone, while those that skip the ONU are mostly short. An operator therefore sets
the two together, $\beta$ for the accuracy the service needs and $\delta$ to save energy either
at no cost in traffic or as much as possible.

  \centering

  \label{fig:delta}
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/delta.pdf}
  \legend{Source: The author}
\end{figure}

\section{Sensitivity}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

WattLadder saves energy in every sensitivity run, which stop at 10,000 households
(Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity}). The more the OLT costs, the smaller the saving, since WattLadder
spends a larger part of its energy at the OLT, 84\% with the broadcast against 66\% under
confidence alone in the main runs (Figure~\ref{fig:energy-split}), so a dearer OLT raises its
energy more: with the GPU card alone, at $\times 1.0$, WattLadder saves
58.9\% with the timetable and 52.8\% with the broadcast, with Google's own accounting, at
$\times 1.72$, 50.1\% and 39.3\%, and with a host that draws as much as its card, at
$\times 3.35$, 31.9\% and 23.0\%, against 43.1\% and 30.4\% at the $\times 2.44$ of the main
runs. At $\times 1.0$ and $\times 1.72$, the timetable again exceeds the target at its lowest
$\beta$, with 83\% and 82\% accuracy. An ONU twice as efficient lowers the saving to 37.2\% and 22.4\%, since confidence alone
then wastes less on the ONU. Charging the same routing under average accounting, which adds the
idle energy to every query's charge, raises it to 46.9\% and 34.0\%.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The results above rest on a few simplifications, gathered here so that the method could be
described without interruptions.

The energies of the phone and the ONU come from recent preprints, the only measurements we
found of these devices running these models, and both have limitations for our use. The
telemetry of \citeonline{cai2026npu} covers the phone's chip but not its memory, so the
phone's real consumption is higher than the 15.7~J per query we charge. It also gives no idle
power, so under marginal accounting the phone is charged its full figure, including whatever
the chip draws at rest, while the ONU and the OLT are charged only their energy above idle.
The first makes the phone look cheaper than it is and the second more expensive, and we found
no measurement of the same phone and model that would tell how far the two cancel.

No commercial ONU runs an LLM today, so the ONU is represented by a single-board computer with
a neural accelerator, the class of device an AI-capable home gateway could carry.
\citeonline{renney2026cloud} measured this board with a plug-in meter on short answers, and its
idle draw of about 3.5~W is an approximate figure~\cite{tummalapalli2026edge}. Reading a prompt
is charged on the assumption that the board draws the same power while reading as while
generating, which neither source measured. The board is also slow, so any query the ONU answers
takes about 44 seconds for an answer of the mean length, which would be cumbersome in
interactive use. This comes mostly from the board rather than from the policy, since its
generation is limited by how fast the Hailo-10H reads its memory~\cite{tummalapalli2026edge}.
A home gateway with faster memory would therefore answer sooner, although we found no
measurement of one.

The factor of 2.44 that brings the OLT's GPU to the whole system combines Google's shares of
a prompt's energy, measured on servers that share one host among several
accelerators~\cite{elsworth2025measuring}, with the average PUE of data
centers~\cite{uptime2025survey}, so it does not necessarily reflect the server and the
building of a real OLT, whose central office may be more or less efficient. The same factor
is kept under marginal accounting, where Google's share for spare machines would not
strictly apply. Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} shows how the results change with the other
factors of Section~\ref{sec:accounting}, including a host that draws as much as its card, since
a server with a single small card shares its host among fewer accelerator watts and could exceed
2.44. The GPU itself
is an L4 held at its power limit of 72~W at every batch size, so a larger batch costs it a lower clock rather than more power. A larger GPU
that is below its limit with a single query would draw more as its batch grows and gain
less from batching, so the OLT's figures hold for a small power-capped card like ours. We also
assume that the hardware stays on when it is idle, so an operator that powers down idle GPUs
would save idle energy that our figures do not count.

WildChat's users chose to try a free chatbot, and a user there is an address and a browser
rather than a household, so they may not chat as a typical household does. Its authors also
note that a service hosted on Hugging Face and shared on Reddit likely draws mostly from the IT
community and those forums, which may not reflect the general population~\cite{zhao2024wildchat}.
WildChat's pauses run from one answer to the next, so they include the time the next answer
took. The drift comes from BurstGPT instead, and its duration $\tau$ is fitted to consecutive
hours, so it matches how much of a departure BurstGPT keeps one or two hours later. In the
data, though, 40 to 50\% of a departure is still there four to eight hours later, while the
model of the mild drift keeps a fifth of it or less.

The simulation reproduces how the OLT's cost changes over time, following the load of the
households and its drift, and holds the other quantities WattLadder reads still. The user
device and the ONU spend a fixed energy per token, while a real phone or gateway spends more
or less depending on its temperature, its battery and whatever else it is running. Every
household has the same three models and asks questions drawn at random from the same
benchmark, so the answer lengths each tier publishes barely move, while real households
would differ in their models and in what they ask. WattLadder uses one mean answer length per
model, although in our runs on GSM8K the standard deviation of a single answer's length is 30
to 40\% of that mean, and the length could be predicted from the query itself~\cite{zheng2023response}.
A follow-up's answer is also the one recorded for its question alone, even though its prompt
carries the conversation. The results therefore show what the policy does when the main
uncertainty it faces is the OLT's load.


%==============================================================================
\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}
%==============================================================================

This work integrated energy awareness into recursive tiered LLM inference through WattLadder,
which we studied over a PON, with the user's phone, the ONU and the OLT as the three tiers. At
the same accuracy, WattLadder spends less energy than escalation by confidence alone wherever
the OLT has enough load to batch its queries, and the saving grows with that load, reaching about
half at 20,000 households. This saving comes from no longer spending the phone's and the ONU's
energy on queries that end at the OLT anyway, and from sending queries to the OLT while it
batches. Weighing energy therefore matters most where a tier batches, since there the cost of a
query depends on the load, which a rule based on confidence alone ignores.

WattLadder also shortens the wait for an answer, since fewer queries pass through the slow ONU.
It carries somewhat more traffic between the tiers, and the margin $\delta$ can remove even
that in exchange for part of the energy saving.

Of the two ways of learning the OLT's cost, the timetable saves more in most of our scenarios,
since it leads the households to keep the OLT busy and cheap. The broadcast does better when
drift moves the OLT between batching and idling, and since it needs no training, it also suits
an OLT whose number of clients changes, where a timetable would first have to be relearned.

As future work, the two strategies could run together, with households following the timetable
and turning to the broadcast's live report when the load departs from its usual pattern. This
hybrid and both strategies would be worth testing on scenarios our study leaves out. Sudden and
deep drops in load, such as a failed PON port or a power cut, would show how much the live report
is worth, and a timetable that is out of date or shared across OLTs would show how quickly its
advantage fades. The hierarchy itself could be extended with a cloud tier above the OLT, while
further down, the ONU of a company or a university serves many users and would batch their
queries as the OLT does. Its cost would then change with the load too, and the same strategies
could share it with the tiers below. Finally, for the ONU of a single home, measuring a gateway
with faster memory would show whether it can answer at an interactive pace and would replace
the approximate figures this work uses for it.
